# Candlestick basics

## 1. What You'll Learn

- What the four prices inside a single candle represent, and how to label them.
- How to tell a bullish candle from a bearish one, and what the wicks are showing.
- Why one candle on its own never confirms a reversal or a continuation.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

A candlestick is a compact picture of what price did during one fixed period of time. On a
fifteen-minute chart, each candle represents fifteen minutes. On a daily chart, each candle
represents one day. Learning to read a candle is learning to read that period of time at a glance.

Every candle contains four prices, often written as **OHLC**. The **open** is the first price traded
in that period. The **high** is the highest price reached. The **low** is the lowest price reached.
The **close** is the final price of the period. When you see a candle, you are seeing all four of
those values, and nothing else.

The thick part of the candle is called the **body**. It spans from the open to the close. The thin
lines above and below it are the **wicks**, sometimes called shadows, and they mark the high and the
low. A long upper wick means price travelled up during the period but did not stay there. A long
lower wick means price travelled down and then recovered. Wicks show you the extremes that were
reached and rejected within that period.

A candle is **bullish** when it closes above its open — the body is coloured one way, typically green
or white, and it tells you the period ended higher than it began. A candle is **bearish** when it
closes below its open — typically red or black. A candle with a very small body, where open and close
are almost the same, is often called a doji. It means the period ended roughly where it started, which
is information about indecision rather than about direction.

Here is the part that matters most for a beginner. A single candle is a description of the past, not a
forecast. A bullish candle does not mean the next candle will be bullish. A long-wicked candle at the
top of a move does not guarantee a reversal — it tells you sellers appeared during that period, which
is worth noting and is not the same thing as a confirmed turn. Candlestick patterns are usually
described in groups, and even then they describe what has happened, not what must happen next. Many
well-formed patterns are followed by nothing at all.

Timeframe also changes meaning. A bullish daily candle is a much larger statement about the market
than a bullish one-minute candle, because it summarises far more trading. Before you read any candle,
know what period it covers.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to show you how to describe a candle. Suppose you are
looking at GBP/USD on a fifteen-minute chart during the London session. You pick one candle from
mid-morning and write down its four values.

The candle opened at 1.2620, reached a high of 1.2648, fell to a low of 1.2615, and closed at 1.2642.
Because the close at 1.2642 is above the open at 1.2620, this is a bullish candle. The body spans 22
pips from open to close. The upper wick runs from 1.2642 up to 1.2648, six pips. The lower wick runs
from 1.2615 up to 1.2620, five pips.

In neutral language, what you can say about this candle is: during those fifteen minutes, price moved
up overall, reached a high of 1.2648, dipped to 1.2615, and finished 22 pips above where it started.

What you cannot say is what the next candle will do. The next candle might continue higher, might
retrace the whole body, or might sit inside this candle's range doing very little. This candle is not
a buy signal, and it is not a reversal signal. It is a record. You will sometimes see a candle of this
shape followed by a strong move, and you will just as often see it followed by nothing. Setups of this
shape fail regularly, and any honest description of a trade has to include the possibility of loss.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Treating a single bullish or bearish candle as an entry trigger rather than as one piece of information.
- Reading a candle without checking which timeframe it belongs to.
- Ignoring the wicks and looking only at the colour of the body.
- Assuming a long wick at a high means the market has turned, when it only shows sellers appeared once.
- Jumping between timeframes until a candle appears that supports the trade already decided on.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Read every candle as a description of a period that has already closed, never as a forecast of the one
about to open.

description, is the habit that keeps later analysis honest.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** A candle opens at 1.2620, reaches a high of 1.2648, falls to a low of 1.2615, and closes at 1.2642. What is it, and what does the body represent?

- A. Bearish; the body spans from the high to the low.
- B. Bullish; the body spans from the open to the close.
- C. Bearish; the body spans from the open to the close.
- D. Bullish; the body spans from the low to the high.

**Correct answer:** B

**Why:** The close at 1.2642 is above the open at 1.2620, so the candle is bullish, and the body always runs from the open to the close while the wicks mark the high and low.
