# Trading Psychology

## 1. What You'll Learn

- The emotional traps — fear, greed, FOMO, overconfidence and revenge trading — that cause rule-breaking.
- How a checklist and a journal protect your decisions from your feelings.
- Why a good trade can lose and a poor trade can win, and why process matters more than any single outcome.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

Most beginners assume their problem will be knowledge. In practice, the knowledge in this tier is not
complicated, and the behaviour around it is where accounts are lost. Psychology is not a soft topic
bolted on at the end. It is the reason risk rules exist.

Here are the traps you will meet, in roughly the order they tend to appear.

**Fear** shows up as hesitation — seeing a setup you planned for and not taking it. It also shows up
as closing a position too early because holding it is uncomfortable.

**Greed** shows up as holding past your own target, or increasing lot size after a good week because
the planned size now feels pointless.

**FOMO**, the fear of missing out, shows up as entering late, after a move has happened, because
watching others appear to profit while you are flat feels intolerable. Social media amplifies this
trap most, because you only ever see the winners.

**Overconfidence** shows up after a run of wins, when a plan starts to feel like a formality and you
size by conviction rather than by the arithmetic from the last lesson.

**Revenge trading** is the most destructive. After a loss, the urge to win the money back is strong,
and it produces trades with no reason, at sizes larger than planned, often minutes after the loss.

Two tools defend against all five.

A **checklist** is a short written list of conditions that must be true before you place a trade.
Writing it down removes the decision from the moment. You are not asking "do I feel good about this?"
but "are all five conditions met?" A checklist turns trading into a task with a right answer rather
than a mood.

A **journal** records every trade: setup, entry, stop, target, risk, result, and whether you followed
your own rules. Its most valuable column is the last. It is where you discover that your losses
cluster around a behaviour — trading a quiet session, sizing up after a win, entering without a
checklist.

Finally, the principle that makes the journal worth keeping. **Judge process as well as outcome, and
understand they are not the same thing.** A good trade can lose: the reason was sound, the risk
planned, the stop hit. A poor trade can win: no reason, oversized, no stop, and it happened to work.
Judge yourself only by the result and you reinforce the bad behaviour and punish the good. Trading
carries a real risk of loss, and a single outcome tells you almost nothing about whether your decision
was sound.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used to show how the same chart looks different depending on state of
mind. Suppose a trader has just taken two losses in a row on GBP/USD, each 30 pips, and it is now the
quiet part of the Asian session.

Price has drifted between 1.2650 and 1.2668 for two hours. There is no clear structure, and the spread
is wider than during London. On a neutral reading, this is a quiet range with nothing to act on, and a
sensible conclusion would be to close the platform and return at the London open.

Now imagine the same chart seen by someone determined to recover the 60 pips. The upper edge at 1.2668
starts to look like a breakout waiting to happen. The two losses become evidence they are due a win.
The wider spread becomes a detail not worth checking. They buy at 1.2665 with a lot size larger than
planned and no stop written down.

Notice what has happened. The chart did not change. The numbers did not change. The decision changed
entirely because of the trader's emotional state, created by the previous two trades rather than by
anything on screen. This trader is not analysing — they are looking for relief, and the market does
not provide it on request.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Trading immediately after a loss in order to win the money back.
- Increasing lot size after a winning streak because the planned size now feels too small.
- Entering late because a move is already underway and being left out feels worse than losing.
- Abandoning the checklist once a few trades have gone well, and calling it experience.
- Judging the quality of a decision purely by whether that particular trade made money.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Follow the checklist you wrote when you were calm, because the version of you watching a live trade
should not be trusted to make the rules.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Complete this exercise in the SAGZFX Practice Account, which uses virtual money only. No real order is
placed and no real funds are at risk.

First, write a personal trading checklist of no more than five conditions that must all be true before
you place a trade. Keep each one checkable, so that the answer is yes or no rather than a matter of
opinion.

Second, write a short plan for what you will do after two consecutive losses. It must be specific:
state how long you will stop trading, what you will review, and what has to be true before you resume.

Third, write the column headings for a trade journal — setup, entry, stop, target, planned risk,
result, and one column headed "did I follow my rules?" Then record at least three demo trades in it,
including any that lost, and fill in the final column honestly for each one.

*Why this matters:* the rules you write while calm are the only rules you will have available when you
are not, and the journal is where you find out which of your rules you actually follow.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** A trade follows your checklist exactly, risks the planned amount, and loses. How should you judge it?

- A. As a bad trade, because a good trade would have made money.
- B. As a good trade that lost, because the process was sound and the outcome is separate from the process.
- C. As proof that the checklist does not work and should be changed immediately.
- D. As a reason to increase your lot size on the next trade to recover the loss.

**Correct answer:** B

**Why:** Process and outcome are separate — a well-planned trade can lose and a careless one can win — so the decision is judged by whether it followed the plan, not by the result.
