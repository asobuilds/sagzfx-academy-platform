# Support & Resistance

## 1. What You'll Learn

- What support and resistance areas are, and why they are drawn as zones rather than exact lines.
- How to mark a zone using swing highs and swing lows rather than a single price.
- Why a zone can break or change role, and why a break is not a signal by itself.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

Support and resistance are two of the oldest ideas in technical analysis, and they are simpler than
most people make them sound.

**Support** is an area where falling price has previously paused or turned back up. **Resistance** is
an area where rising price has previously stalled or turned back down. That is the whole definition.
It is a description of where price has reacted before, and nothing more.

The first correction to make is that these are **zones, not exact lines**. If you draw support as a
single price to four decimal places, price will frequently trade a few pips through it and then
recover, and you will conclude that support failed when nothing meaningful happened. Draw a band
instead — a small range that contains the relevant highs or lows. Where you draw the edges of that
band involves some judgement, and two experienced traders will often draw slightly different zones
from the same chart. That is expected, and it is why a zone is a note about the past rather than a
precise instrument.

The second correction is about **why** a zone can matter at all. A zone is not a physical barrier.
It is a place where, previously, buying or selling pressure was strong enough to change price's
direction. Market conditions change. The reasons that produced that reaction may no longer exist.
So zones can be broken, and when price moves decisively through a zone, that zone has failed as a
turning point.

Sometimes a broken zone is later revisited from the other side and produces a reaction — resistance
that was broken can later act as support, and the reverse. This is often called a role change or a
retest. It is a real and commonly observed behaviour, and it is also not reliable. A retest can hold,
can break straight through, or can stall with no clear reaction at all.

The practical consequence is that marking a zone tells you **where to look**, not **what to do**. A
zone is a place on the chart worth paying attention to. Whether anything happens there depends on
what price actually does when it arrives, and you only find that out after the fact. A zone is
therefore never an entry signal by itself, and a break of a zone is never a guaranteed continuation.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to show you how to mark zones. Suppose you are looking at
EUR/USD on the H4 chart on a Tuesday. Over the previous three weeks, price has turned down three times
from the area between 1.0960 and 1.0990, and turned up twice from the area between 1.0740 and 1.0770.

On that evidence you would mark resistance as a band from 1.0960 to 1.0990, and support as a band from
1.0740 to 1.0770. Both are drawn from swing points — the specific highs and lows where price reversed
— rather than from a round number chosen in advance.

Now price rises from 1.0800 and reaches 1.0985, inside your resistance band. From here, several things
are possible, and you do not yet know which will happen. Price could stall and turn down again, which
is what happened three times before. It could push through to 1.1010 and keep going. It could spike
to 1.1005, fail to hold, and drop back into the band.

Notice that nothing about the zone itself tells you which of these it will be. The zone tells you this
is a location where price has previously reacted. A trader who decided in advance what they would do
in each of those three scenarios is prepared. A trader who treats the zone as a signal to sell is
guessing, and this setup fails regularly.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Drawing support and resistance as single exact prices instead of zones.
- Redrawing zones after the fact so that they match what price actually did.
- Treating a touch of a zone as an automatic entry rather than as a location to observe.
- Assuming a broken zone will necessarily be respected when price returns to it.
- Marking so many zones that almost any price level is covered by one, which makes them meaningless.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Mark your zones before price arrives at them, because a zone drawn after the reaction is a description
of the past rather than a plan.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Complete this exercise in the SAGZFX Practice Account, which uses virtual money only. No real order is
placed and no real funds are at risk.

Open the H4 chart for EUR/USD and choose a historical period of at least three months. Working from
left to right, mark your zones as you go rather than after seeing the whole chart.

1. Mark two support zones and two resistance zones, each as a band rather than a single line.
2. For each zone, write down which specific swing highs or lows you used to set its edges.
3. For each zone, count how many times price reacted there before your chosen period ended.
4. For each zone, note at least one occasion where price traded through it and did not react.

Finally, write two sentences explaining why one of your zones could reasonably be drawn differently by
another trader, and what that tells you about how much weight the zone deserves.

*Why this matters:* marking zones honestly, including the ones that failed, is what turns this tool
from a source of false confidence into a genuine description of where price has reacted.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** Which statement best describes a support zone?

- A. A precise price at which price is guaranteed to stop falling.
- B. An area where falling price has previously paused or turned back up, and which can later break.
- C. A level that proves large institutions have placed buy orders there.
- D. A level that only exists on the daily timeframe.

**Correct answer:** B

**Why:** Support is a zone drawn from where price has previously reacted, it can fail or change role, and it says nothing about which participants placed orders there.
