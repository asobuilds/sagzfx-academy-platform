# How to read a simple chart

## 1. What You'll Learn

- A repeatable sequence for reading any chart: context, structure, levels, scenarios and a no-trade condition.
- How to describe what price is doing in clear, neutral language without predicting the outcome.
- Why every chart reading should end with a condition under which you would not trade at all.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

This lesson assembles the Beginner tier into one routine. Reading a chart is not a moment of insight;
it is a short, ordered checklist you repeat until it becomes automatic.

**Step one: context.** Check the instrument and the timeframe before anything else. Which pair are you
looking at, what does one candle represent, and which sessions does this chart cover? Reading a
five-minute chart in the Asian session is a different exercise from reading the daily chart, and
confusing the two is where most beginner analysis goes wrong. Note also whether a major news release
falls inside the period, because that changes how much weight the price action deserves.

**Step two: structure.** Identify the recent swing highs and swing lows. Are they rising, falling, or
roughly level? From that, describe the market in one sentence: it is behaving like an uptrend, a
downtrend, or a range. Use words honestly — "unclear" is a valid answer and often the right one.

**Step three: levels.** Mark the zones where price has previously reacted — support bands below and
resistance bands above, drawn from swing points rather than round numbers. Mark a range's boundaries
if there is one. These are the locations worth your attention.

**Step four: scenarios.** For each location that matters, describe what you would need to see in order
to act, and what you would need in order to stand aside. A scenario is a conditional statement, not a
forecast. "If price closes above the upper band, the range description is no longer valid" is a
scenario. "Price will break out" is not.

**Step five: the no-trade condition.** Before you finish, state at least one condition under which you
would not trade at all. This is the step beginners skip and the one that protects the account. Common
conditions: structure is unclear, the spread is wider than usual, a major release is due within the
hour, or the location is not one you planned for.

Two rules run through all five steps. First, **describe before you search for an entry**. The order
matters: look for a trade first and you will find one, built on whatever the chart happens to support.
Second, write in **neutral language**. If a sentence would change depending on what happens next, it
is a prediction and does not belong in a chart reading.

Trading carries a real risk of loss, and no reading tells you what price will do next. A disciplined
reading tells you what you would do in each case, which is the only part you control.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used to show the sequence in action. Suppose it is a Wednesday morning
and you open GBP/USD on the H4 chart, covering two weeks, with your session bands marked.

**Context:** GBP/USD on H4, so each candle is four hours, with no major release scheduled during the
London session. **Structure:** swing lows at 1.2410, 1.2475 and 1.2530; swing highs at 1.2560 and
1.2620. Both are rising, so the honest description is an uptrend on this timeframe. **Levels:**
resistance band 1.2660 to 1.2690, from two previous turning points; support band 1.2530 to 1.2560,
from the most recent swing low and the area above it. **Scenarios:** if price returns to 1.2550 and
holds above the support band, the uptrend description remains intact and the location is worth
watching. If price closes below 1.2530, the higher-low sequence has failed. If price rises into 1.2660
and stalls, nothing is confirmed either way. **No-trade condition:** if price sits between 1.2580 and
1.2640 in a narrowing range with no clear reaction at either band, there is nothing to act on.

Read that back. Nowhere does it say what price will do. Every statement describes the past or plans
conditionally for the future, and the reading ends by naming a situation in which the answer is to do
nothing. That is a complete chart reading: it can be wrong about direction without being wrong about
process.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Looking for an entry before describing what the chart is doing.
- Skipping the instrument and timeframe check, and reading a five-minute chart as if it were a daily.
- Writing predictions into the reading and calling them analysis.
- Finishing without ever stating a no-trade condition.
- Changing the reading every few minutes, so that no plan survives contact with the market.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Read the chart in five steps, describe before you search, and always end by naming the condition under
which you would not trade.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Complete this exercise in the SAGZFX Practice Account, which uses virtual money only. No real order is
placed and no real funds are at risk.

Choose three pairs and, for each one, write a full chart reading using the five-step sequence: context,
structure, levels, scenarios and no-trade condition. Use a different timeframe for each pair — one on
M15, one on H1 and one on H4 — so that you practise adjusting the reading to the timeframe.

For each reading, keep to the following rules:

1. Context: the pair, the timeframe, what one candle represents, and whether a major release falls in the period.
2. Structure: summarised in one sentence using uptrend, downtrend, range or unclear.
3. Levels: at least one support band and one resistance band, with the swing points noted.
4. Scenarios: written as "if this, then that" statements, with no predictions.
5. A no-trade condition specific enough that another student could check whether it occurred.

*Why this matters:* a chart reading that ends in a stated no-trade condition is one you can follow
without needing the market to agree with you.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** According to the five-step sequence, what should you do immediately before searching for a possible entry?

- A. Decide which direction you expect price to move.
- B. Describe the instrument, timeframe, structure and key levels in neutral language.
- C. Increase your lot size to make the trade worthwhile.
- D. Check what other traders are posting about the pair.

**Correct answer:** B

**Why:** The sequence is context, structure, levels, scenarios and a no-trade condition — description comes before any search for an entry, so that the entry is built on the chart rather than the chart being read to fit an entry.
