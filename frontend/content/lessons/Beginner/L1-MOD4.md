# Buy/Sell & order types

## 1. What You'll Learn

- What it means to buy or sell a currency pair, and why a sell is not a bet against the market.
- The difference between market, limit and stop orders, and when each one is used.
- Why stop orders can slip in fast markets, and what that means for the price you actually get.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

Every forex trade has two sides, and you are always doing both at once. When you buy EUR/USD you are
buying euros and selling dollars. When you sell EUR/USD you are selling euros and buying dollars.
There is no separate "short" market, and selling is not an unusual act — it is simply the other
direction of the same pair.

A **buy** position generally benefits when the price of the pair rises. A **sell** position generally
benefits when the price falls. Notice the word "generally". A position does not earn anything by
existing. It only changes in value as price moves, in either direction, for as long as you hold it.

Now to orders. An order is your instruction to your broker about how and when to enter or exit. There
are three types you need to know.

A **market order** asks to be filled immediately at whatever price is currently available. You get
speed and certainty of execution, but not certainty of price. In normal conditions the fill is very
close to what you saw on screen. In fast conditions it may not be.

A **limit order** asks to be filled only at a specified price or better. If you want to buy at 1.0950
and price is currently 1.1000, a buy limit at 1.0950 waits until price falls to that level. You get
control over price, but no guarantee of a fill — price may never reach your level, and the
opportunity simply passes.

A **stop order** becomes active only when price reaches a trigger level you set. Beyond that trigger
it usually behaves like a market order. This is the crucial detail beginners miss: a **stop-loss is a
stop order**, so when it triggers it becomes a market order, and market orders can be filled at a
worse price than requested. That gap is called **slippage**, and it is most likely around major news
releases and at the weekly open, when liquidity is thin and price is jumping.

The practical consequence is that your stop-loss is a boundary you have chosen, not a promise about
the largest loss you can take. Plan for the possibility that your exit is a few pips or more away
from where you placed it. Trading carries a real risk of loss, and slippage is one of the ways a loss
can exceed what you expected.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to show how the order types differ. Suppose it is a
Thursday morning during the London session and you are watching GBP/USD on a one-hour chart. Price is
currently 1.2650.

You have identified 1.2600 as a level where price has previously paused on the way down. One approach
would be to place a buy limit at 1.2600, so that you only enter if price comes back to that level. If
price instead rises from here and never returns, your order simply sits unfilled and nothing happens.
That is a normal outcome, not a failure.

An alternative approach would be to place a buy stop at 1.2680, entering only if price breaks upward
through that level. You would be accepting a worse entry price in exchange for waiting for the market
to move first.

Either way, you would need to decide in advance where you would admit you were wrong — perhaps 30 pips
from your entry — and where you would consider the idea complete. And either way, the setup can fail.
Price can reach your entry and reverse to your stop. Price can trigger your stop order during a news
release and fill several pips beyond it. Nothing about placing the order tells you which of these
will happen.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Believing a sell position is somehow riskier or more advanced than a buy position.
- Using market orders by default because they fill instantly, then paying for it in fast conditions.
- Placing a stop-loss and assuming the fill will be exactly at the level chosen.
- Placing a limit order and treating it as a pending trade that is certain to happen.
- Leaving an order resting in the market through a major news release without deciding whether that is intended.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Know which type of order you are placing and what it does at the moment it triggers, because that is
the moment you will not be able to change your mind.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Complete the following in the SAGZFX Practice Account, which uses virtual money only. No real order
is sent to any broker and no real funds are involved.

Open the order ticket for EUR/USD and read every field before filling anything in: order type, lot
size, entry price, stop-loss, take-profit and any expiry field. Write down in your own words what each
field controls.

Then, on paper, build three hypothetical trades for GBP/USD at a current price of 1.2650, one using
each order type:

1. A market buy, with a stop 30 pips below your assumed entry and a target 60 pips above it.
2. A buy limit 50 pips below the current price.
3. A buy stop 30 pips above the current price.

For each one, write a sentence stating the condition under which it would not be filled at all, and a
sentence stating what could cause the exit to occur at a price worse than planned.

*Why this matters:* most early losses come from misunderstanding what happens at the moment an order
triggers, not from picking the wrong direction.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** Which statement about a stop-loss order is correct?

- A. It guarantees you will be filled at exactly the price you set.
- B. It is a limit order, so it fills only at your price or better.
- C. It is a stop order, so once triggered it becomes a market order and may fill at a worse price.
- D. It only works during the London session.

**Correct answer:** C

**Why:** A stop-loss activates at your trigger level and then behaves as a market order, which is why slippage around news and thin liquidity can push the fill beyond the level you chose.
