# Pips, lots, spreads & leverage

## 1. What You'll Learn

- What a pip is, and how to read one on both non-JPY and JPY pairs.
- How lot size describes position size, and why the same stop distance risks different amounts.
- How the spread and leverage affect every trade you take.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

Four numbers decide what a trade actually costs you and what it can do to your account. Beginners
often learn to read a chart before they learn these, which is the wrong order.

A **pip** is a standard unit of price movement. On most pairs it is the fourth decimal place, so a
move from 1.1000 to 1.1001 is one pip. On pairs quoted in Japanese yen it is the second decimal
place, so a move from 150.20 to 150.21 is one pip. Some brokers also show a fifth decimal, called a
pipette or fractional pip, which is one tenth of a pip. Learn to count in pips, because every risk
decision you make later is measured in them.

A **lot** describes position size. A standard lot is 100,000 units of the base currency, a mini lot
is 10,000, a micro lot is 1,000 and a nano lot is 100. This matters because the value of one pip
changes with your lot size. On EUR/USD, roughly ten dollars per pip on a standard lot becomes roughly
one dollar on a mini lot and ten cents on a micro lot. The chart does not know your lot size; your
lot size decides what the chart's movement is worth to you.

The **spread** is the difference between the ask and the bid. If the bid is 1.1000 and the ask is
1.1002, the spread is 2 pips. You pay it on entry, because a buy opens at the ask and a sell opens at
the bid. That means every trade begins slightly behind, and the market has to move in your favour by
at least the spread before you are level. The spread is not a fee you can avoid; it is a cost you
manage.

**Leverage** lets you control a large position with a small deposit. If your broker offers 1:100, a
100 dollar deposit can control a position of 10,000 dollars. **Margin** is the deposit your broker
sets aside as collateral for that position. Margin is not free money and it is not a bonus — it is
your own money held as security.

Leverage magnifies gains and losses equally. A move that would have gained 10 dollars unleveraged can
gain or lose 1,000 dollars at 1:100. This is why most beginners who lose an account do not lose it by
misreading a chart. They lose it by taking a position far too large for their balance. Trading
involves a real risk of loss, and leverage is the tool that turns a manageable loss into a
catastrophic one.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These numbers are hypothetical and exist only to show you how to do the arithmetic. Suppose you are
looking at EUR/USD on a one-hour chart during the London session. Price is at 1.1000 bid and 1.1002
ask, so the spread is 2 pips.

You consider a buy. Your entry would be at the ask, 1.1002. You decide that if price reaches 1.0972
your reason for the trade no longer holds, so your stop would sit 30 pips below your entry.

Now the position sizing question. On a micro lot, 30 pips is roughly 3 dollars of risk. On a mini lot
it is roughly 30 dollars. On a standard lot it is roughly 300 dollars. The chart, the entry and the
stop are identical in all three cases. Only the lot size changes what you are risking.

Notice how much of this is decided before the trade is placed, and how little of it depends on being
right. It is entirely possible that price never reaches your stop and never reaches any target
either, drifting sideways for hours before you close it manually. It is equally possible that price
falls to your stop and beyond. A 30-pip stop is a boundary you chose, not a guarantee that the loss
stops there; in fast markets, execution can slip past it.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Measuring profit in money rather than pips, so that a large lot size feels like a bigger opportunity.
- Forgetting that a buy opens at the ask, and so under-counting the distance price must travel.
- Treating margin as extra spending power rather than as your own money set aside.
- Using maximum leverage because the minimum deposit is small and the position feels harmless.
- Sizing a position by how confident you feel instead of by the distance to your stop.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Set your stop first and let the distance to it decide your lot size, never the other way around.

Work through this in the SAGZFX Practice Account, which uses virtual money only. No real order is
placed and no real funds are at risk at any point in this exercise.

Pick EUR/USD and USD/JPY in the Market Watch window. For each pair, write down the current bid and

count your pips differently.

Next, choose a hypothetical stop distance of 25 pips. Using the contract specifications shown in the
platform for each pair, calculate what 25 pips would be worth in US dollars on a micro lot, a mini

Finally, write one sentence for each pair explaining which lot size you would choose if your plan
allowed you to risk no more than 10 dollars on the trade.

*Why this matters:* this is the arithmetic that separates a trader who controls risk from one who
hopes. It takes five minutes and it is the single most useful habit in this tier.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** If the bid on a pair is 1.1000 and the ask is 1.1002, what is the spread, and when do you pay it?

- A. 2 pips, paid only when you close a profitable trade.
- B. 2 pips, paid on entry because buys open at the ask and sells open at the bid.
- C. 0.2 pips, paid once per day as a financing charge.
- D. 2 pips, paid only if the trade is held overnight.

**Correct answer:** B

**Why:** The spread is ask minus bid, and because you enter at the less favourable of the two prices, you start every trade that much behind.
