# Trendlines & market direction

## 1. What You'll Learn

- How to tell an uptrend, a downtrend and a range apart using swing highs and lows.
- How to draw a trendline from swing points, and why it is a subjective tool.
- Why a trendline should never be used on its own as a reason to enter a trade.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

Before you look for a trade, you need to answer one question: what is this market doing? There are
three broad answers, and most charts are giving you one of them at any given time.

An **uptrend** is a sequence of higher highs and higher lows. Price pushes up to a new peak, pulls
back, and the pullback stops above the previous low. An uptrend is defined by that pattern repeating,
not by a single strong candle.

A **downtrend** is the mirror image: lower highs and lower lows. Each rally fails below the previous
peak, and each decline reaches below the previous low.

A **range** is when neither of those is happening. Price moves sideways between an upper boundary and
a lower boundary, and the highs and lows are roughly level rather than rising or falling.

A **swing high** is a peak where price turned down, and a **swing low** is a trough where price turned
up. Structure is read from these turning points, which means you need more than one of each before you
can call a direction. One higher high on its own is not a trend. Two or three in sequence, with higher
lows between them, is a meaningful description of what the market is doing.

A **trendline** is a straight line drawn along the swing points of a move. In an uptrend you connect
the rising swing lows; in a downtrend you connect the falling swing highs. Its purpose is a simple
visual summary of the move's pace and direction.

Here is the honest limitation. Trendlines are **subjective**. Where you start the line, and which
swing points you choose to connect, changes the slope and changes where the line sits. Two traders
looking at the same chart will often draw two different trendlines, and neither is objectively wrong.
A line drawn through the wicks tells a different story from one drawn through the bodies. This is not
a flaw you can fix with more precision; it is a property of the tool.

The practical consequence is that a trendline should never be used **alone** as a reason to enter a
trade. "Price touched my trendline" is a statement about a line you drew yourself. It becomes useful
only alongside other evidence — structure, a zone, a defined trigger — and even then it describes what
has happened rather than what will happen. A trend can end without warning: a trendline reliable for
weeks can break in a single session.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to show you how the classification is made. Suppose you are
looking at GBP/USD on the H4 chart over a six-week period during the London and New York sessions.

Working from left to right, you note the swing points. Price rises to 1.2510, pulls back to 1.2440,
rises to 1.2585, pulls back to 1.2505, then rises to 1.2660. The highs are 1.2510, 1.2585 and 1.2660
— each higher than the last. The lows are 1.2440, 1.2505 and a third low you mark provisionally at
1.2560. On that evidence, the honest description is: this is behaving like an uptrend, with higher
highs and higher lows.

You draw a trendline connecting the swing lows at 1.2440 and 1.2505 and extending it to the right.
Depending on which wicks you use, the line could reasonably be drawn a few pips higher or lower than
where you placed it.

Now price falls back towards that line. From here, several outcomes are possible. Price could touch
the line near 1.2565 and turn up again, continuing the pattern of higher lows. It could slice through
the line and keep falling, which would put the uptrend description in question. It could also stall
in a narrow range for days, making the whole classification unclear.

The trendline tells you roughly where the previous rising lows sat. It does not tell you that price
will respect the line, and this setup fails regularly. Anyone who tells you a trendline touch is a buy
signal is describing a pattern, not a probability.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Calling a trend after a single higher high without waiting for a higher low to confirm it.
- Redrawing the trendline each time price breaks it, so that it always appears to have held.
- Using a trendline touch as a complete trade reason with no other evidence.
- Reading direction from one timeframe and ignoring that a higher timeframe shows a range.
- Forcing a trendline onto a range, where a horizontal boundary describes the market better.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Describe the market's direction in words before you draw a single line, because a trendline should
illustrate your description rather than create it.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Complete this exercise in the SAGZFX Practice Account, which uses virtual money only. No real order is
placed and no real funds are at risk.

Open the H4 chart for three pairs — EUR/USD, GBP/USD and USD/JPY — and choose the same three-month
historical window for each. Then, for each chart:

1. Mark the five most recent clear swing highs and five most recent swing lows.
2. Write one sentence classifying the market as uptrend, downtrend or range, and justify it using the specific swing points.
3. If you classify it as a trend, draw one trendline and note which swing points you connected.
4. Write one sentence describing a condition under which your classification would no longer be valid.

Finally, write short notes on two of the three charts stating why another trader could reasonably draw
a steeper or shallower trendline from the same price data.

*Why this matters:* classifying direction in words first, and naming the condition that would prove
you wrong, keeps a subjective tool in its proper place.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** Which sequence describes a downtrend?

- A. Higher highs and higher lows.
- B. Lower highs and lower lows.
- C. Highs and lows that are roughly level.
- D. A single large bearish candle on the daily chart.

**Correct answer:** B

**Why:** A downtrend is defined by the repeating pattern of lower highs and lower lows, while roughly level boundaries describe a range and one candle alone never establishes a trend.
