# Entry confirmation

## 1. What You'll Learn

- What a trigger is, and why it must be written in exact, checkable language.
- Two common confirmation families — a close beyond a swing, and displacement followed by a retracement.
- Why changing the trigger rule after the outcome makes a journal useless for review.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

Entry confirmation is the event that converts a watched area into an executed trade. Without it, you are
not trading a setup — you are entering on hope, at whatever moment the waiting becomes uncomfortable. A
trigger is what makes the entry a decision you made rather than a decision the market made for you.

The first requirement is that the trigger be written in numbers. "A one-hour candle closes above 1.0905"
is checkable by anyone, including you tomorrow. "A strong close" and "a clean break" are not checkable;
they are adjectives, and adjectives get defined after the fact. If the rule cannot be checked by someone
reading your journal next month, it is not a rule.

Two confirmation families cover most approaches. The first is **a close beyond a swing**: price closes
past a specific swing level on a specific timeframe. The close is what matters, because a wick through a
level describes a momentary excursion rather than a change in where price is being accepted. The second
is **displacement followed by a retracement**: a large, decisive candle leaves a gap or imbalance in its
wake, and price then returns partway into that candle before continuing. Both families share the same
logic — wait for evidence that the level is being respected, then act on the evidence rather than on the
anticipation.

The enemy of both is retrofitting. Once the outcome is visible, you know which rule would have worked,
and it is remarkably easy to "discover" that rule afterwards. A rule written after the fact is not a
rule; it is a description, and a strategy assembled from descriptions is a strategy fitted to the past.
Write the version you will use, with a timestamp, before the session opens — and if you change it, start
a new record rather than editing the old one.

The purpose of a trigger is to trade the same way twice. That is all it can do, and nothing about it
tells you whether the trade will work.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to show how a trigger is written and applied. Suppose you are
watching AUD/USD on a Monday, and your top-down analysis has identified a one-hour demand area between
0.6580 and 0.6590.

Before the session opens, you write the trigger: "a one-hour candle closes above 0.6605; if that
happens, I look for a retracement into the 0.6595 to 0.6600 area and only then consider an entry. If no
one-hour close above 0.6605 occurs, there is no trade, regardless of how the candle looks intraday."

Now suppose price trades down into the area at 0.6585 during the London session. Your rule has not yet
been satisfied or failed — it is waiting on the close of the one-hour candle. If that candle closes at
0.6601, there is no trigger, and no trade. That is the rule working as designed, not the rule failing.
A rule that fires on every touch is not a confirmation rule; it is an entry rule wearing a confirmation
label.

Notice what the example does not tell you. It does not say price closed above 0.6605. It does not say
the retracement came. And even if both had happened, the entry could still have been stopped out —
confirmation is evidence that a level is being respected, not proof of what comes next. Setups that meet
their trigger fail every day, and any honest account of a method says so plainly.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Entering when price merely touches the area, then describing the touch as confirmation.
- Writing the trigger in adjectives — strong, clean, convincing — rather than in numbers and a timeframe.
- Adding a second, looser trigger mid-trade so a losing entry can be relabelled as valid.
- Taking confirmation from the wrong timeframe, such as a five-minute close validating a four-hour idea.
- Grading a trigger rule by whether the trade won rather than by whether it was followed exactly.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Write the trigger in numbers before the session opens, and follow the version you wrote even when a
different rule would have worked.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. This account uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed,
and nothing you do here can earn or lose you real funds.

Write two trigger rules in exact language, one from each confirmation family — for example, "a four-hour
candle closes above the 1.0920 swing high" and "a displacement candle on the one-hour followed by a
retracement into the 0.6595 to 0.6600 area." State the timeframe, the level, and the closing requirement
for each.

Then find three historical examples on a chart where your conditions were met or clearly not met, and
record for each one whether the rule fired, whether the entry would have been taken, and — separately —
whether the rule was written before or after you knew the outcome. Score yourself on the last point
only.

*Why this matters:* a trigger that has been checked against three past examples and timestamped is a
rule; a trigger that has only ever been checked against trades you remember is a story.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** Your written trigger is "a one-hour candle closes above 0.6605." Price trades up to 0.6603
during the hour and closes the candle at 0.6601. What is the correct action?

- A. Enter, because price came within two pips of the level.
- B. Enter, and record the trigger as met but the level as marginally missed.
- C. Do not enter — the written condition was not met, and the rule stands as written.
- D. Lower the trigger to 0.6601 so the rule matches what price did.

**Correct answer:** C

**Why:** A trigger is checkable only if it is applied exactly as written; adjusting the level to match
what price did is retrofitting, and it destroys the journal's value for review.
