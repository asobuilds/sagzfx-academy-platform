# Journaling

## 1. What You'll Learn

- The fields a complete journal entry contains, and why each one exists.
- Why process and outcome are graded on separate lines rather than merged into one result.
- How to draw one usable lesson from an entry instead of a list of regrets.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

A trading journal is the record that makes review possible. Without one, every conclusion you reach about
your own trading is drawn from memory, and memory edits the chart: it keeps the trades that felt
significant, drops the ones that felt routine, and quietly rewrites the reasoning you had at the time.
The journal exists to hold the version you cannot revise.

A complete entry records, at minimum: the date and session; the instrument and timeframe; the
higher-timeframe context; the setup you were trading; the entry, stop and target; the position size and
the planned cash risk; the result; the costs — spread, commission and swap; a screenshot taken at entry;
your emotional state at entry and at exit; and whether every written rule was followed.

Two of those fields deserve explanation. The **screenshot** matters because a chart you reconstruct from
memory is not the chart you traded; the image is what stops the record from drifting. The
**emotional state** field is a data field, not a diary. "Anxious after three consecutive losses, so I
halved the size" is a checkable description. "The market was against me" is not, and it belongs nowhere
in a professional record.

The most important structural decision is that every trade is graded twice. Once for **outcome** — what
it made or lost — and once for **process** — whether you followed the written plan, answered as
followed, partly followed, or not followed. These two grades disagree constantly, and the disagreements
are the most valuable rows in the journal. A followed plan can lose, and that is not evidence the plan
is wrong. A broken plan can win, and that is the single most dangerous entry you can record, because it
teaches the wrong lesson with the authority of a profit.

Finally, keep one lesson per entry. An entry that ends with ten regrets produces none of them, because
nothing specific gets carried into the next session.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to show the shape of an entry. Suppose a student logs a
GBP/USD short taken on 12 March during the London session.

The record reads: four-hour context bearish, price at a one-hour supply area; entry 1.2780; stop 1.2810;
target 1.2720; position size derived from a planned $10 risk on a $2,000 practice balance; spread 1.1
pips; no commission on the practice account. The screenshot shows the supply area and the trigger candle.
Emotional state at entry: calm, the trigger was written the previous evening. Result: stopped out at
1.2810.

The two grades are then written on separate lines. **Outcome:** loss of the planned amount plus costs.
**Process:** followed — the trigger rule was met before entry, the stop was not widened, and the exit was
taken at the written level rather than improvised. The single lesson: "the plan was executed correctly; a
single loss is not evidence that the plan is wrong."

Now consider the same trade recorded differently, and note how much the lesson changes. Suppose the
student had widened the stop to 1.2830 mid-trade, and price had later reached 1.2720. **Outcome:** win.
**Process:** failed — the stop was moved, which was not in the plan. The lesson here is not "widening
works"; it is "a rule-breaking trade produced a profit, and that is the outcome most likely to corrupt
the next decision." Without the two separate grades, this entry would have been filed as a good trade.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Recording only the winners, or only the trades that felt significant at the time.
- Writing entries from memory days later, so the chart in the journal is the chart as remembered.
- Grading only the outcome, so a rule-breaking winner is filed as a good trade.
- Using the emotional-state field for venting rather than for a description someone else could check.
- Listing ten lessons in one entry, which reliably guarantees that none of them is acted on.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Grade every trade twice — once for what it made or lost, and once for whether you followed the plan — and
believe the second grade more.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. This account uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed,
and nothing you do here can earn or lose you real funds.

Fill in a sample journal with five hypothetical trades. For each one, complete every field: date and
session, instrument and timeframe, context, setup, entry, stop, target, size, planned cash risk, result,
costs, emotional state at entry and at exit, and whether the written rules were followed. Note that a
practice account has no real position behind it, so the emotional-state field should describe what you
observed in yourself during the exercise.

Then write two separate grades for each trade — one for outcome and one for process — and one lesson per
entry. Finish by counting how many of your five entries have a process grade of "followed". That
percentage, not the five results, is the number to carry into the next assignment.

*Why this matters:* the process grade is the only part of the journal that describes something you
control, and separating it from the result is what stops a lucky rule-breaker from being recorded as a
good decision.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** A student enters a trade, moves the stop wider than the written plan allowed, and the trade
later reaches its original target. How should this be recorded?

- A. As a good trade, since it produced a profit.
- B. As a rule-breaking trade: outcome win, process failed, with the lesson being that the profit does not validate the change.
- C. As a rule-followed trade, since the target was the one originally written.
- D. It should be left out of the journal, because the exception is unlikely to recur.

**Correct answer:** B

**Why:** Outcome and process are graded separately, and a profit produced by breaking the plan is the
most misleading entry a journal can hold — the process grade is what preserves the lesson.
