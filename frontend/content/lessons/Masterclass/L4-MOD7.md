# TP targeting

## 1. What You'll Learn

- Four defensible sources for a target: prior swings, range boundaries, a planned R multiple, and a tested exit rule.
- Why the obstacles between entry and target decide whether the reward actually justifies the risk.
- Why a target is a plan to be reviewed, never a promise about price.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

A take profit is a written exit decision made before entry. Like the stop, it exists so that the exit is
a decision you took rather than a reaction you had. There are four defensible places to get one.

**Prior swings.** A previous swing high or low is a price where the market already turned once, so it is
a location with a reason behind it rather than a number pulled from the air. **Range boundaries.** If you
are trading inside a range, the far edge is a natural destination and is often where the trade's logic
completes. **A planned R multiple.** Here you decide in advance that you will aim for a set multiple of
your risk — two times, three times — wherever that lands on the chart. This has the advantage of being
unambiguous and easy to test, and the disadvantage of ignoring structure. **A tested exit rule.** Any
exit condition you have validated across a historical sample belongs in this category, and it carries
more weight than an untested preference.

Whichever source you use, the next step is the one traders skip: look at what sits between entry and
target. If the path crosses a supply area, a swing high, or the edge of a range, then your target is
optimistic and the reward-to-risk ratio you wrote down is not the one you will experience. The ratio
itself is worth being precise about — it is the distance to the target divided by the distance to the
stop. It is a statement about the geometry of your plan. It is not a probability, and quoting it as
though it were is one of the most common ways traders mislead themselves.

None of this promises anything. A plan with a favourable ratio loses often, and it is supposed to,
because the ratio describes the shape of the trade rather than the likelihood of it. A target is a plan,
not a promise — and the same applies to partial exits, which are a management decision and belong in the
written plan rather than being invented once the trade is moving.

One detail that quietly matters: if your target ignores spread, commission and swap, the reward you
recorded is larger than the reward you would actually receive.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to show how a target is chosen and checked. Suppose you are
considering a short on GBP/USD with an entry at 1.2780 and a stop at 1.2810 — 30 pips, which on a $2,000
practice balance risking 0.5% represents $10.

Three candidate targets exist on the chart. A prior swing low at 1.2690, which produced a sharp move
away from it. A range floor at 1.2660, tested several times. And a planned 2R target, which at 30 pips of
risk would sit 60 pips below entry, at 1.2720.

Checking the obstacles: the path from 1.2780 down to the 2R level at 1.2720 does not cross the 1.2690
swing low, because 1.2720 sits above it. So the 2R target is reachable without first passing through the
more obvious structural level, which makes it the least obstructed candidate. The 1.2690 swing low is the
natural first destination and the obvious place for a partial exit, written in advance. The range floor
at 1.2660 only becomes a realistic target if 1.2690 breaks and holds below, which is a separate condition
rather than part of this plan.

Notice what the example does not tell you. It does not say price reached any of those levels. It does not
say the trade worked. It says only which exits were defensible before the outcome was known — which is
the only moment at which choosing an exit is a decision rather than a description.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Picking a target because it is a round number or a satisfying ratio rather than a chart feature or a tested rule.
- Setting a target without checking what sits between the entry and the target.
- Quoting a reward-to-risk ratio as though it were a probability of the trade succeeding.
- Moving the target further away once the trade is in profit, so the plan changes with the emotion.
- Ignoring spread, commission and swap, so the recorded reward is larger than the one actually received.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Write the target before the entry, name the obstacles in between, and treat it as a plan that can be
reviewed rather than a promise that can be relied on.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. This account uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed,
and nothing you do here can earn or lose you real funds.

Set targets for two hypothetical trades. For each one, write the entry, the stop, and the target, and
state which of the four sources you used — prior swing, range boundary, planned R multiple, or tested
exit rule. Calculate the reward-to-risk ratio from the distances you wrote, and label it as a plan
ratio, not a probability.

Then check the path between entry and target and list every obstacle in the way: a supply area, a swing
high, a range edge. If an obstacle sits in the path, either move the target to the near side of it or
write down the condition under which the trade would continue past it. Finish by writing the partial
exit rule, if any, in the same pass.

*Why this matters:* the obstacle check is what separates a reward-to-risk ratio that describes the chart
from one that describes a hope, and writing partial exits at the same time keeps them from being invented
mid-trade.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** You plan a short with a 30-pip stop and a 60-pip target, and a major swing low sits 45 pips
below your entry, directly in the path. What is the most accurate assessment?

- A. The plan is a 2R trade, and the swing low is irrelevant because the target is beyond it.
- B. The 2R ratio overstates the realistic reward, since price must pass through a structural level before reaching the target.
- C. The target must be widened so that the swing low is no longer in the path.
- D. The stop should be widened so the ratio remains 2R after the obstacle is accounted for.

**Correct answer:** B

**Why:** Reward-to-risk is calculated from plan distances, but an obstacle between entry and target
means price must clear a structural level first, so the recorded ratio is more optimistic than the
realistic one — the target should be reconsidered or made conditional.
