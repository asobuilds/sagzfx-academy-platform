# Market bias

## 1. What You'll Learn

- Why a bias is a conditional statement about evidence rather than a prediction about direction.
- How to write bullish, bearish and neutral scenarios for one chart without contradicting yourself.
- What evidence would invalidate each scenario, and how to say so before the market decides.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

A market bias is what you currently expect, given the evidence on the chart. The word *given* is doing
the work. A bias is conditional: it holds while certain things remain true, and it stops holding when
they do not. It is not a forecast, and it is not a certainty. Traders who treat it as either end up
defending a view instead of reading a chart.

The practical way to write a bias is to state three scenarios rather than one — bullish, bearish and
neutral — and for each one, the evidence that supports it and the evidence that would invalidate it.
This is not fence-sitting. Fence-sitting is refusing to say anything. Writing three scenarios is the
opposite: it commits you to naming exactly what you would need to see for each one to become the live
case. A bias with no invalidation is not a bias, it is a belief.

A bias also needs a horizon. "Bearish on the daily over the next two weeks" is a checkable statement.
"Bearish" alone is not, because it does not say on what chart, over what period, or against what
evidence. And a bias is not a signal. A bearish bias does not mean sell now; it means you are
interested in supply areas rather than demand areas, and that you will read triggers in one direction
more seriously than the other. The entry still has to be earned by a trigger.

Neutral deserves particular respect. Charts spend a great deal of time going nowhere, and "neutral while
price holds between these two levels" is frequently the most accurate sentence available. Traders who
cannot write a neutral bias are usually traders who cannot tolerate not having a position.

Finally, honesty about uncertainty is not weakness in a briefing. A short market briefing that says
"this is what I expect, this is what supports it, and this is what would prove me wrong" is more useful
to the person hearing it than any amount of confident certainty, because it can be acted on and checked.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to show how a conditional bias is written. Suppose you are
looking at EUR/USD on the daily chart, with price at 1.0860.

**Evidence for a bearish case:** the daily has printed lower highs since 1.1050, and price has been
holding below a shelf at 1.0950 for three weeks. **Evidence for a bullish case:** a higher low formed at
1.0780 last month and has not been broken, and the weekly chart is still ranging rather than trending
down. **Evidence for a neutral case:** price has spent the last three weeks between roughly 1.0800 and
1.0950 without closing outside either edge.

The written bias then reads something like this: "Bearish while daily closes remain below 1.0950. The
bearish case weakens on a daily close above 1.0950 and is void above 1.1050. The bullish case requires a
daily close above 1.0950 first, and strengthens if 1.0860 is reclaimed and held. Neutral while the
1.0800 to 1.0950 range holds."

Notice what this does not say. It does not say price will fall. It does not assign a probability to any
scenario, and it does not tell you to buy or sell anything — the trigger lessons come later. It says what
you expect, on what evidence, for how long, and what would change your mind. A bias written this way can
be reviewed honestly afterwards, because it named the conditions in advance.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Writing a single directional bias and dismissing the other two scenarios as unlikely without stating evidence.
- Confusing bias with an entry signal, and buying or selling the moment the bias is formed.
- Letting a bias survive every piece of contrary data by reinterpreting the evidence each time.
- Stating a bias with no horizon and no invalidation level, so it can never be shown to be wrong.
- Presenting a briefing as certainty because uncertainty feels unprofessional to say out loud.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

State your bias as a condition — what you expect, what evidence supports it, and the price that would
prove it wrong.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. This account uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed,
and nothing you do here can earn or lose you real funds.

Choose one major pair and one timeframe. Write a short market briefing of no more than one paragraph,
covering all three scenarios: bullish, bearish and neutral. For each scenario, give the evidence that
supports it and the specific price or event that would invalidate it. Name the timeframe and the horizon
explicitly.

Then read the briefing back and check one thing: does any sentence tell you to place a trade? If it
does, rewrite it, because a bias describes what you expect and never instructs you to act.

*Why this matters:* a bias that names its own invalidation in advance is the only kind that can be
reviewed honestly later, and it is the input that every later decision in the plan depends on.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** A trader writes: "EUR/USD is bearish, so I am selling." What is the main problem with this
statement?

- A. Nothing — it is concise and gives a clear direction.
- B. It states a bias as certain, gives no evidence, names no invalidation, and treats the bias as an entry signal.
- C. It is wrong because the correct bias on EUR/USD is always neutral.
- D. It is acceptable as long as the trade is small.

**Correct answer:** B

**Why:** A bias is a conditional expectation supported by evidence and bounded by an invalidation level;
it is not a certainty and it does not by itself authorise an entry.
