# Top-down analysis

## 1. What You'll Learn

- How to give each of three timeframes one job instead of letting them compete for attention.
- Why a conflict between timeframes is recorded as a fact rather than resolved by changing charts.
- How to write a top-down analysis that another trader can follow from the highest timeframe down.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

Top-down analysis means starting at the largest view of the market and working downwards, so that each
decision is made inside a context that is already established. You use three timeframes, and each one
has exactly one job.

The **higher timeframe** supplies context. This is where you decide what kind of market you are in — a
trend, a range, or something unclear — and roughly where price sits inside that structure. The **middle
timeframe** supplies the setup zone: the specific area where you would be willing to do business. The
**lower timeframe** supplies the trigger: the event that turns a watched area into an executed trade.
A common arrangement is daily for context, four-hour for the zone, and one-hour or fifteen-minute for
the trigger, though the exact ladder matters less than the fact that each rung has one job.

The failure this structure prevents is subtler than it sounds. Most traders do not skip timeframes; they
shop across them. They form a view on the daily, dislike it, drop to the four-hour, find something more
appealing, and then drop again until a chart agrees with the trade they already wanted. Nothing about
the market changed — only the window they were looking through. A top-down analysis is done once, in
order, and the answer it produces is the answer you keep.

That is why conflicts must be recorded rather than resolved. If the daily leans bearish while the
four-hour is turning up from a demand area, both statements can be true at the same time, and neither is
wrong. What you write down is the conflict itself, together with the condition that would settle it:
"the bearish daily read is only actionable below this level; above it, the four-hour turn takes
precedence." Written that way, the conflict becomes a plan. Left unwritten, it becomes a decision you
make later, under pressure, with a position already open.

More timeframes do not produce more information. They produce more options, and options are what allow a
conclusion to be reverse-engineered.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to show the process. Suppose you are analysing GBP/USD on a
Tuesday morning.

**Higher timeframe — weekly.** Price has spent two months moving sideways between roughly 1.2500 and
1.2900, currently around 1.2640. There is no clean trend to inherit.

**Middle timeframe — daily.** Since a high at 1.2900, the daily has printed lower highs, the most recent
at 1.2780. On its own, that leans bearish.

**Setup zone — four-hour.** Price is sitting inside a four-hour supply area between 1.2650 and 1.2680.
Underneath it, a daily demand shelf sits near 1.2600, which is also close to the floor of the weekly
range.

Here is the conflict, and it is the whole point of the exercise: the daily leans bearish, but price is
standing on a demand shelf just above range support. The honest written version is not a direction. It
is a condition — "the bearish daily read is actionable on a four-hour close below 1.2600; while 1.2600
holds, this is a range and both directions are live." The fifteen-minute chart has no trigger to offer
yet, and that is a legitimate result.

Notice what the example does not tell you. It does not say price broke 1.2600. It does not say it
bounced from the shelf. Either was possible, and the analysis was written to cover both without
predicting either.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Starting on the five-minute chart because that is where the movement is, then working upwards to justify the conclusion.
- Using one timeframe for both context and trigger, so there is no top-down structure at all.
- Re-running the analysis on a different timeframe whenever the first answer was "no trade".
- Leaving a conflict unrecorded, then discovering it only after a position is open.
- Treating three agreeing timeframes as confirmation of an outcome rather than as a consistent description.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Give each timeframe one job, and when two of them disagree, write the disagreement down instead of
changing the chart.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. This account uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed,
and nothing you do here can earn or lose you real funds.

Choose one pair and complete a top-down worksheet on it. Name three specific timeframes and, for each
one, write a single sentence: what the higher timeframe says about context, what the middle timeframe
says about the setup zone, and what the lower timeframe would need to do to trigger a trade.

Then add a fourth line headed "Conflict". If the three timeframes agree, write "none" and say why. If
they disagree, state the disagreement and the exact price or event that would resolve it. Do this once,
in order, and do not revisit it on a different timeframe afterwards.

*Why this matters:* recording the conflict in writing is what stops the analysis from being re-run until
it produces a preferred answer, and the resolving condition you write here is the same level you will
later use for invalidation.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** Your weekly chart is ranging, your daily chart leans bearish, and your four-hour chart is
turning up from a demand area above range support. What is the correct top-down output?

- A. Abandon the analysis and wait until all three timeframes agree on direction.
- B. Use the daily view, because the highest timeframe with a direction always wins.
- C. Record the conflict and write the condition that resolves it, such as a four-hour close below range support.
- D. Switch to a fifteen-minute chart and follow whichever direction it shows.

**Correct answer:** C

**Why:** Timeframes describe different windows of the same market and can legitimately disagree, so the
professional output is the recorded conflict plus the condition that would settle it — not a direction
chosen by changing charts.
