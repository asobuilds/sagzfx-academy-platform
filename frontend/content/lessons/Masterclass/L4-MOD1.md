# Putting Everything Together (Capstone)

## 1. What You'll Learn

- The eight parts of a complete trade plan, and why each one is written before entry rather than during it.
- Why a missing condition is a no-trade decision, not an invitation to improvise.
- How to present a full setup so another trader can check every decision you made.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

In the earlier tiers you learned the parts separately: reading structure, marking levels, waiting for a
trigger, sizing a position, managing a trade, reviewing it afterwards. This capstone is about the order
they go in, and about the fact that every one of them is decided before you click anything.

A complete trade plan has eight parts. **Context** is the higher-timeframe picture — what the market has
been doing over the period you care about. **Structure** is the sequence of swings that describes the
current leg. **Location** is the specific area where you are willing to do business: a zone, a level, a
gap. **Trigger** is the exact event that turns a watched area into an executed trade. **Invalidation** is
the price that proves you wrong, and it is chosen before the trigger, never after. **Size** is the
position that makes your invalidation cost exactly the amount you decided to risk. **Management** is what
you will do at targets, at breakeven, and if the trade stalls. **Review** is the record you keep
afterwards, graded on process rather than on outcome.

The order matters, because each part constrains the next. If you choose size first, the stop gets forced
into a place the chart never justified. If you choose the target first, the trigger tends to be loosened
until it fires. Written in order, the plan becomes a chain of dependent decisions rather than a
collection of preferences.

The most useful property of a written plan is that it can be incomplete. If context is unclear, or
structure conflicts with location, or no valid trigger appears, the correct output is no trade — and you
only notice that when the parts are laid out separately. Improvisation hides the gap; a checklist
exposes it.

None of this removes risk. A complete plan can still lose, and it will lose regularly. Forex and
leveraged trading carry a real risk of losing some or all of your trading capital. What the plan changes
is that a loss becomes a known, budgeted cost instead of a surprise.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to show how the eight parts fit together. Suppose EUR/USD is
trading at 1.0920 during the London session.

**Context:** the daily chart has printed higher lows since 1.0800. **Structure:** on the four-hour, the
sequence is higher highs and higher lows, with the most recent higher low at 1.0885. **Location:** a
four-hour demand area between 1.0890 and 1.0900 that overlaps a level price broke upward last week.
**Trigger:** a one-hour candle closing back above 1.0905 after price taps that area. **Invalidation:** a
one-hour close below 1.0880, which is underneath both the area and the higher low. **Size:** on a
$2,000 practice balance risking 0.5%, the planned cash risk is $10; the distance from a 1.0905 entry to
a 1.0880 stop is 25 pips, and the position size is whatever makes 25 pips equal $10 once you have
verified your broker's contract specifications. **Management:** a partial exit at the prior swing high
near 1.0960, with the remainder trailed behind each new four-hour higher low. **Review:** log the trade
whether it triggers or not.

Notice what this example does not tell you. It does not say price returned to the area. It does not say
the trigger fired. It does not say the trade won. Price could have run to 1.0960 without ever coming
back, and if the setup had triggered, the stop could have been hit. A plan describes the conditions you
will act on; it says nothing about what happens next.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Writing the plan after the entry, then presenting it as though it came first.
- Treating a missing condition as "close enough" because the setup looks appealing.
- Choosing position size before locating the invalidation level, so the stop lands wherever the size allows.
- Editing the trigger wording after seeing the outcome, so a losing entry can be relabelled valid.
- Presenting a setup to a mentor or a group with the failure scenarios left out.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

A trade is not ready until all eight parts are written down and the invalidation is a price, not a
feeling.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. This account uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed,
and nothing you do here can earn or lose you real funds.

Choose one pair and write a complete eight-part plan for it — context, structure, location, trigger,
invalidation, size, management and review — before you look at what price did afterwards. Do not place
the trade. If a part cannot be completed, write "no trade" next to it and state which condition was
missing.

Then present the plan to another person, or record yourself reading it aloud, covering the losing
scenarios as carefully as the winning one. Finish by naming the single part you found hardest to write,
because that is the part you have been skipping.

*Why this matters:* the capstone skill is not finding setups, it is refusing the ones that are missing a
part, and that refusal only becomes possible when the parts are written separately.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** You have context, structure, location, trigger and invalidation written for a setup, but you
cannot decide on a target because a four-hour supply area sits directly above your entry. What does a
complete trade plan require here?

- A. Enter anyway with a wide target, since five of the eight parts are ready.
- B. Resolve the target and management rules first, and if the reward does not justify the risk, record it as a no-trade.
- C. Skip the target entirely and decide it once the trade is in profit.
- D. Enter with a smaller position so the missing target matters less.

**Correct answer:** B

**Why:** Every part of the plan is written before entry, and a missing or unjustifiable part is a
no-trade decision rather than something to resolve while the position is already open.
