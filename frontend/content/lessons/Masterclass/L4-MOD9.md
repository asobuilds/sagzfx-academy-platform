# Trade management

## 1. What You'll Learn

- Why partial exits, trailing stops and invalidation exits must be written before the entry.
- Why widening risk or inventing a new rule mid-trade breaks the only record that can be reviewed later.
- How to role-play the three standard paths a trade takes: to target, back to entry, and to the stop.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

Trade management is everything you do after the order is live. It covers four decisions, and all four
belong in the plan before the entry rather than in your head while the position is open.

**Partial exits.** Whether you close part of the position at a level, and what fraction. **Trailing
stops.** Whether the stop moves, and by what written rule — behind each new higher low on a named
timeframe, or to breakeven once a defined distance has been covered. **Invalidation exits.** What you do
if the structure your idea depended on breaks while the stop has not been touched. **Time and event
exits.** What you do if the trade simply stalls, or if a scheduled news release is approaching.

The reason all of this is written first is that mid-trade you are working under two disadvantages at
once. You are under pressure, and the information available to you is asymmetrical: you can see the
trade moving but not where it is going. Rules invented under those conditions are shaped by hope when
the trade is winning and by fear when it is losing. Neither is a strategy.

Of all the mid-trade decisions, widening the stop is the most destructive, because it converts a
planned, budgeted loss into an unbudgeted one, and it does so at exactly the moment the market is
telling you that your idea was wrong. Moving the stop to breakeven is less dramatic but not harmless:
done too early it converts a trade that needed room into a scratch, and a strategy that scratches
constantly is a strategy that pays costs for nothing.

It helps to know in advance that a trade has only three standard paths. It reaches the target, it
returns to entry, or it reaches the stop. If you have written your response to all three before the
entry, then every path has an answer ready, and none of them requires a decision made under stress.

None of this improves the outcome of any individual trade. Management reduces the number of decisions
you make while under pressure, and that is the whole of what it offers.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to show a written management plan. Suppose you take a short on
USD/CAD with an entry at 1.3620, a stop at 1.3590 — 30 pips, representing $10 of planned risk — and a
first target at 1.3680, which is twice the risk distance.

Before the entry, the management plan is written in full. Close half the position at 1.3680. Move the
stop to the entry price on the remainder once that partial has filled. Trail the remainder behind each
new one-hour higher low. Exit the whole position on a one-hour close above 1.3600, even if the stop has
not been touched, because that close would break the structure the idea was built on.

Now role-play the three paths in writing, before anything happens. **Path one — target:** the partial
fills at 1.3680, the stop moves to entry, the remainder trails behind successive one-hour higher lows
until the trail is hit. **Path two — back to entry:** price moves partway toward the target but returns
to 1.3620 before the partial fills, so no stop adjustment has occurred and the trade exits at a scratch
plus costs. **Path three — the stop:** price trades directly to 1.3590 and the position closes for the
planned $10 loss plus costs.

Notice what the example does not tell you. It does not say which path occurred. It does not say the plan
worked. What it shows is that all three answers existed before the trade opened, so none of them had to
be improvised while the position was live.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Widening the stop as price approaches it, because the original one now "feels too tight".
- Inventing a management rule mid-trade and recording it afterwards as though it had been planned.
- Moving the stop to breakeven immediately, so ordinary noise removes a trade that needed room.
- Taking a partial exit at a level that was never written down, then constructing the rationale afterwards.
- Holding a position past its stated invalidation because the stop itself had not been hit.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Every exit — partial, breakeven, trail or invalidation — is decided before the entry, never while the
position is open.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. This account uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed,
and nothing you do here can earn or lose you real funds.

Take one hypothetical trade and write its full management plan before anything else: the partial exit
level and fraction, the rule for moving the stop, the invalidation condition, and any time or event exit.

Then role-play the three standard paths in writing. For each path — reaches target, returns to entry,
reaches the stop — write the exact sequence of actions you would take, in order, including what your
stop is doing at each stage. Do not place the trade; the deliverable is the written plan for all three
paths.

Finish by reviewing your own plan for one thing: does any step require a decision that is not already
written down? If it does, complete it now rather than later.

*Why this matters:* a plan that already answers all three paths cannot be improvised under pressure, and
the paths you find hardest to write are usually the ones you have been deciding emotionally.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** A trade is 20 pips from its stop and moving against you. The structure your idea depended on
still holds, and no written rule has been triggered. What does disciplined management require?

- A. Widen the stop slightly to give the trade more room, since the structure is still intact.
- B. Follow the written plan exactly; any change to the stop or exit must have been written before the entry.
- C. Close the position immediately to avoid the loss.
- D. Double the position size to lower the average entry price.

**Correct answer:** B

**Why:** Management decisions are made before entry precisely because mid-trade decisions are taken under
pressure, and widening a stop converts a budgeted loss into an unbudgeted one.
