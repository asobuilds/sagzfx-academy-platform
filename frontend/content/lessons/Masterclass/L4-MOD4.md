# Liquidity identification

## 1. What You'll Learn

- Which chart features tend to attract attention, and how to mark them consistently.
- Why a liquidity level is a place to watch rather than a target you are entitled to reach.
- How to define the reaction you require at a level before you consider a trade from it.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

In this context, liquidity means the resting orders that cluster around prices everyone can see. Those
clusters form in predictable places, because they are created by predictable behaviour.

The first is the **obvious swing high or swing low** — the turn that stands out on the chart without
needing to be pointed out. Stops tend to sit just beyond it, because that is where traders place them.
The second is **equal highs or equal lows**, where price has reached roughly the same level two or more
times. A double top looks like a wall, and a wall invites orders to be placed in front of it and stops
to be placed behind it. The third is the **range edge** — the boundary a market has repeatedly
respected, which becomes the line everyone watches for a break.

Marking these is straightforward. What matters is what you do next, and the discipline is this: a
liquidity level is a place to watch, not a target you are entitled to reach. Price does often trade
toward these areas, because that is where activity is concentrated. But "often" is a description of
tendency, not a guarantee about this instance. There is no rule that a pair must reach the equal highs
you have marked, and a level that has not been reached has not told you anything.

So the level must be separated from the signal. Marking is the first task; the second is writing down the
reaction you require before you would consider the level tradeable. For example: "if price sweeps above
1.0920 and closes back below it on the one-hour, rejection — I will look for a setup back inside the
range. If it closes above 1.0920 and holds on a retest, the level has been accepted, and I stand aside
from shorts." Two traders can mark the identical level and take opposite trades, and both can be
internally consistent, because their required reactions differ.

One last point worth holding onto: an obvious level is obvious to everyone, including the participants
on the other side of your trade.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to show the marking and the reaction rule. Suppose you are
studying USD/JPY on the four-hour chart.

Three features stand out. First, equal highs at 149.80, touched twice about six weeks apart. Second, an
obvious swing low at 148.10 that produced a sharp move away from it. Third, a range floor at 147.20,
tested three times over the same period.

You mark all three and write a required reaction for each one. For 149.80: "a four-hour close above
149.80 would suggest acceptance, so I would look for continuation on a retest. A wick above 149.80 with a
four-hour close back below 149.30 would suggest rejection, so I would look for a setup back toward the
148.10 swing low. No reaction means no trade."

For 147.20 the reaction rule is different, because it is a range floor rather than a level inside the
range: "a four-hour close below 147.20 breaks the range, and I will re-analyse from the higher timeframe
rather than treating it as an automatic entry."

Notice what the example does not tell you. It does not say which of those reactions occurred, or that
any of the levels was reached. It could have drifted sideways between 148.10 and 149.80 for a week. The
output of this lesson is three marked levels and three written conditions, not a position.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Treating an equal high as a magnet that price is obliged to reach, rather than a place to watch.
- Marking twenty levels so that one is always nearby when a trade needs justifying.
- Entering at the level itself instead of waiting for the reaction you said you required.
- Rewriting the reaction rule after the sweep, so it matches whichever direction price actually took.
- Forgetting that a level visible to you is equally visible to the participants trading against you.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Mark the level, then write the reaction you need — a level without a defined reaction is not a trade.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. This account uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed,
and nothing you do here can earn or lose you real funds.

Choose one pair and one timeframe. Use two colours: one for liquidity reference areas, and a second for
everything you consider a signal. Mark three liquidity reference areas — for example an obvious swing
high, an area of equal lows, and a range edge — and place nothing in the signal colour yet.

Beneath the chart, write one required reaction for each marked area, in the form: "if price does X at
this level, I look for Y; if it does Z, I stand aside." Be specific about the timeframe the reaction must
close on.

*Why this matters:* separating the two colours makes the distinction physical rather than theoretical,
and writing the reaction in advance is what stops a level from being retrofitted into a signal after
price has already moved.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** You have marked an obvious swing high where stops are likely resting. Which statement
describes the correct use of that level?

- A. Price will reach it, so a position can be opened in anticipation.
- B. It is a reference area; it becomes tradeable only when the reaction you defined in advance occurs there.
- C. It should be ignored, because obvious levels carry no information.
- D. It can be used as an entry as soon as price touches it, without further conditions.

**Correct answer:** B

**Why:** Liquidity areas mark where activity tends to concentrate, which is a tendency rather than a
guarantee, and the level alone is not a signal until a pre-defined reaction occurs.
