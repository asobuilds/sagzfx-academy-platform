# Correlation Analysis

## 1. What You'll Learn

- How to tell pairs that move together from pairs that move against each other, and why the US dollar is usually the common thread.
- What risk-on and risk-off behaviour describes, and why the same position can behave differently depending on which one is active.
- Why several open positions can be one bet in disguise, and how to count risk per idea rather than per position.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

Correlation is the word for how two instruments move relative to each other over a chosen period. It is
measured on a scale from minus one to plus one. A reading near plus one means the two have tended to move
in the same direction over that window; a reading near minus one means they have tended to move in opposite
directions; a reading near zero means no dependable relationship in either direction. The most important
thing to understand about correlation is what it is not: it is a description of the past over a window you
chose, not a promise about the next hour.

In the currency market, most pairs contain the US dollar. EUR/USD, GBP/USD, AUD/USD, NZD/USD and USD/JPY
together account for the large majority of turnover, and the dollar appears in nearly all of them. This is
why the dollar is often described as the common thread. When the dollar strengthens broadly, most pairs
quoted as dollars-per-something tend to fall together and USD/JPY tends to rise. When the dollar weakens,
the reverse tends to happen. So a group of positions that all sit on one side of the dollar is one position, expressed several times,
paying several spreads.

Pairs that share a currency without sharing the dollar also correlate. AUD/USD and NZD/USD both price a
currency against the dollar and react to similar global conditions, so they often move together. EUR/USD and USD/CHF often move against each other, because the franc and the euro
frequently trade in the same direction against the dollar. Both statements describe tendency, not law.

Risk-on and risk-off are shorthand for the broad appetite for risk across markets. In a risk-on
environment, money tends to favour currencies tied to growth, commodities and higher interest rates, and
tends to leave the currencies seen as safer. In a risk-off environment, that flow tends to reverse.
The practical point is that your position does not exist on its own. It sits inside a
regime, and the same pair can be helped or hindered by which regime is currently in force.

The core lesson of this module follows from all of that. Risk must be counted per idea, not per position.
If your plan allows one percent of your account at risk per trade, then three correlated positions, each
risking one percent, are not three separate risks. They are closer to a single idea carrying three percent.
Counting per idea is what stops a routine-looking set of positions from becoming one large bet by
accident.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to illustrate the process.

Suppose it is the New York session and you hold three open positions: long EUR/USD at 1.0950 with a stop
forty pips away, long GBP/USD at 1.2720 with a stop fifty pips away, and long AUD/USD at 0.6650 with a stop
thirty pips away. On a per-position view, each is a separate trade with its own chart, and each looks
modest on its own.

Now write down the currency exposure line by line. All three are long a non-dollar currency and short the
dollar. Three charts, one common denominator. If the dollar strengthens during the session, all three are
tested at the same time, and the stops sit closer together in time than the three separate price levels
suggest. Each one still has its own stop distance, but they are all children of the same move.

If each position risks half a percent of the account, the group is not three half-percent risks with three
separate chances of being wrong. It is one dollar idea carrying
about one and a half percent, tested once.

The relationship can break, and that cuts both ways. A domestic event in the euro area or the United
Kingdom can move one pair while the others barely respond, which is exactly the situation where a
correlation you assumed stops describing the market.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Treating a measured correlation as permanent, then sizing three positions as if they were independent.
- Counting risk per position in a plan while thinking about the trades as separate ideas.
- Reading a high correlation as a trading signal, rather than as an exposure warning.
- Assuming risk-on and risk-off are permanent states instead of descriptions of current flow.
- Checking correlations once, writing them into a plan, and never measuring them again.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Before you count your positions, count your ideas — if several trades share one currency exposure, they are
one bet and must be sized as one.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. It uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed, and nothing you
do there can earn or lose you real funds.

Place four positions across different major pairs, including at least two that you expect to move together.
Then open your position list and rewrite every entry by hand as a currency exposure, for example "long euro,
short dollar" rather than "long EUR/USD".

Group the four into ideas. For each group, add the individual risk percentages together and write the total
in the margin. If a group total exceeds the risk limit you have set for a single idea, reduce the positions
until it does not. Record in one sentence which position you deliberately cut and why.

*Why this matters:* converting positions into currency exposures is the only reliable way to see that a
tidy-looking list of trades can be a single concentrated bet, and the grouping exercise makes the total
risk visible before the market makes it visible for you.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** You hold long EUR/USD, long GBP/USD and long AUD/USD, each sized to risk half a percent of the
account, and your plan allows one percent of risk per idea. What does the module say about this book?

- A. It is fine, because each position stays under the one percent per-trade limit.
- B. It is diversified, because three different pairs cannot fall together.
- C. It is one dollar idea carrying about one and a half percent, so total risk per idea exceeds the limit.
- D. It is fine as long as the three positions have different stop distances.

**Correct answer:** C

**Why:** All three positions are short dollar against three different currencies, so they express one idea,
and risk is counted per idea by adding the individual position risks together rather than reviewing each in
isolation.
