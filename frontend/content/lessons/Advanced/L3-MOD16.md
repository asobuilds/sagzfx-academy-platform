# Trading Psychology at Professional Level

## 1. What You'll Learn

- How to grade a trading day by whether you followed your written process rather than by what any single trade produced.
- Where the tilt and revenge cycle begins, and the pre-committed rule that ends your day before it does damage.
- Why boredom is a real trading risk, and how a written routine turns deliberate practice into something repeatable.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

The professional problem is subtler: your judgement is a variable, and it changes with what the market has just done to you.

Process orientation means judging a session by whether you executed your written plan. Outcome orientation means judging the same session by whether it produced a profit. The two point in different directions constantly: a correctly executed plan can still lose, and a rule you broke can still, by luck, pay. If your sense of a good day tracks the money, you are training yourself to repeat whatever happened to be profitable, including the mistakes.

The clearest failure mode is tilt. Tilt is the state where a decision is driven by the need to repair a recent loss rather than by your plan. The tilt and revenge cycle usually runs the same way: a loss stings, the next setup is judged too generously because you want the money back, then a rushed entry becomes a second loss, which raises the emotional stakes again. This is why the exit must be decided in advance. A pre-committed stop-trading rule is a written condition — a number of losses, a percentage of your risk budget, or a fixed time — at which your day ends regardless of how you feel about the next setup.

Boredom trading is the quiet version of the same problem. When nothing in your plan has appeared for a while, the urge to be doing something starts to feel like analysis, and a setup you would not normally take becomes acceptable. That is a personal condition, not a market one: a session should be something you can end early rather than fill.

Drawdowns change judgement in ways that are hard to notice from inside them. After a run of losses, risk that was tolerable last week feels unbearable, and the temptation is to either stop taking valid setups or to take them far too large to recover quickly. Both are the same error: letting a number change the rules instead of the rules governing the number. If your identity depends on being right, a losing trade is an attack rather than a cost of doing business, and that is when rules get broken to avoid the feeling.

The professional answer is deliberate practice: choosing one specific skill, rehearsing it under your written routine, and reviewing the result against a standard you set before you began.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

The numbers below are hypothetical, and the walkthrough deliberately stops at the decision point.

Suppose you trade GBP/JPY on the fifteen-minute chart during the London session. Your written plan defines a setup: a sweep below the Asia-session low followed by a close back above it, with the Asia low marked at 187.40. Price trades to 187.22, then closes back above the level at 187.48, and your plan records an entry at 187.48 with a stop at 187.10 and a target at 188.30.

Suppose the stop at 187.10 is reached and the position is closed. The walkthrough does not resolve it, because your next decision is not a chart question but a routine question: your plan either contains a pre-committed condition that ends the session here, or it does not.

On one, the rule fires, you close the platform, and you write two lines in your journal. On the other, the loss is re-read as a reason the setup "almost worked", a second entry is taken within minutes, and it is larger than plan because the first one needs repairing. The chart does not tell you the second is a mistake; only the written rule, made when you were calm, does.

The example leaves the outcome open. A trader on either path could have finished the week up or down, which is exactly why the process, not the result, has to be the standard you grade against.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Reviewing the day by the profit and loss figure first and the adherence notes second, or not at all.
- Setting a daily stop-trading rule in the morning and negotiating with it after the second loss.
- Taking a setup you would have skipped, because the session has been quiet and something feels overdue.
- Increasing size after losses so the book gets back to even faster.
- Treating a run of losses as a verdict on your ability, then changing a working rule to relieve the feeling.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Let the day be judged by whether you followed what you wrote while calm, never by what the market happened to pay you.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. It uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed, and nothing you do there can earn or lose you real funds.

Write a one-page trading routine before your next session. It must contain four stated items: the session hours you will trade, the exact setup you are permitted to take, your maximum risk per trade, and a pre-committed stop-trading condition written as a specific number or time.

Then run ten sessions against that page. For each, log the number of trades, whether each conformed to the permitted setup, and whether the stop-trading condition was reached and honoured. At the end, grade all ten sessions on adherence only, and write one paragraph on any session where your behaviour and plan diverged, naming the trigger you noticed at the time.

*Why this matters:* separating the adherence grade from the profit figure is what lets you see your own behaviour clearly, and a stop-trading condition is only useful if it was written before the moment it had to be used.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** You follow your written plan exactly on three trades, all of which lose at the stop, and the session ends on your pre-committed stop-trading condition. How should the day be graded?

- A. As a failed day, because the plan lost money on every trade.
- B. As a successful day, because the process was followed and the pre-committed exit condition was honoured.
- C. As a day that proves the setup no longer works and the rule should be changed immediately.
- D. As neither, since a single session carries no information at all about anything.

**Correct answer:** B

**Why:** Process adherence is the standard you can control and review, and three losing trades executed to plan say nothing about whether the plan was followed correctly.
