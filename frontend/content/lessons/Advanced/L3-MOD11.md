# Advanced Multi-Timeframe Setups

## 1. What You'll Learn

- How to nest three timeframes into a higher-timeframe narrative, a mid-timeframe zone, and a lower-timeframe trigger.
- What written alignment rules look like, and how they produce the same decision every time rather than a fresh judgement each session.
- What to do when the timeframes disagree, instead of waiting for an agreement that may never arrive.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

In the Market Structure tier you met multi-timeframe analysis as a way of checking agreement: a higher
timeframe gave you direction, a lower timeframe gave you an entry. That is still the foundation. What it
does not settle is how you choose the timeframes, which one owns the decision, and what you do when the
two of them are not saying the same thing. Those three questions are what this module is about.

The first shift is from agreement to nesting. Instead of two timeframes that either agree or do not, you
work with three, and each one has a single defined job. The higher timeframe sets the narrative: the broad
story of where price sits relative to recent structure, and whether the market is trending or ranging. The
mid timeframe holds the zone: the specific area you would want to act from if the story is right — a region
you marked before you started watching, not one you draw once price arrives. The lower timeframe provides
the trigger: the small, recent event that says the moment has arrived. Narrative, zone, trigger. Each layer
answers one question, and no layer is allowed to answer a question that belongs to another.

A workable ratio to write into your plan is roughly one to four to sixteen. If your narrative timeframe is
the daily, your zone timeframe is the four-hour and your trigger timeframe is the one-hour. The exact
numbers matter less than the relationship: each step down shows the previous timeframe in finer detail, so
a level that looks like a single line on the daily becomes a band with its own internal history on the
four-hour.

Written alignment rules turn this from a way of looking into a way of working. You state in advance the
condition each layer has to meet. For example: the narrative must be trending in a stated direction; the
zone must already be marked on my chart; the trigger must be a named event, such as a lower-timeframe close
back above a stated level. If all three conditions are true, the setup is valid. If one is missing, it is
not, and wanting it to be true does not change the count.

The hard part is disagreement, and disagreement is normal rather than exceptional. The higher timeframe can
trend up while the mid timeframe sits in a range. The zone can be a long way from current price. The
trigger can fire at the wrong end of that range. The professional response is not to wait for universal
agreement, which may never come, but to hold a written priority order. A defensible one is this: the
narrative sets direction, the zone sets location, the trigger only times the entry. In that ordering, a
missing trigger delays the decision, a missing zone cancels it, and a contrary narrative removes it
entirely.

Notice what the structure does. It gives every layer both a job and a veto, so a conflict produces a
decision instead of a mood. That is the whole point of nesting, and it is the difference between a method
and an improvisation.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to illustrate the process.

Suppose you are studying EUR/USD during the London session, with the daily as your narrative timeframe. On
the daily, structure is trending up: higher lows at 1.0820 and then 1.0875, with price currently around
1.0945. That is your narrative condition, and it is met.

Your zone timeframe is the four-hour. Before the session you marked a zone between 1.0890 and 1.0910,
where an earlier imbalance and a previous consolidation overlap. Price has not reached it yet, so the zone
condition is not yet met.

Your trigger timeframe is the one-hour. You wrote the trigger in advance: a one-hour close back above
1.0920 after the candle has wicked into the zone. That event has not happened either.

Three conditions, one met. On your written rules, there is nothing to act on yet, and the correct output
for the session is to wait and watch. That is a complete answer, not a failure of the method.

Suppose instead that price did reach the zone and the one-hour close followed. All three conditions would
be satisfied, and the setup would be valid by your rules. Valid does not mean correct. The setup can still
fail: the daily narrative would only be invalidated by a close below the 1.0875 higher low, and price is
free to do that without ever asking your permission. Nesting improves the quality of your decisions; it
does not remove the possibility of loss.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Choosing your timeframes to suit the answer you already want, then calling the result analysis.
- Drawing the zone after price arrives in it, which quietly removes the only condition that tests patience.
- Letting the trigger timeframe override the narrative, so a small reversal wipes out a larger plan.
- Waiting for all timeframes to agree before acting, which in practice means never acting.
- Running a different checklist order each session, so you cannot compare one week's decisions with the next.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Give each timeframe one job and one veto, and let a written priority order — not the last chart you looked
at — settle any disagreement between them.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. It uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed, and nothing
you do there can earn or lose you real funds.

Write a one-page top-down checklist with three numbered lines: narrative condition, zone condition, trigger
condition. Choose one major pair and fix your three timeframes using the one-to-four-to-sixteen ratio.

Then annotate twenty past sessions of that chart without looking ahead, running the checklist in the same
order each time. For every session, record one of three outcomes: the checklist was not met, the checklist
was met, or the checklist was met and the daily narrative was later invalidated. Count how many sessions
fell into each group.

*Why this matters:* the count tells you how often your rules actually permit a decision, and running the
same order twenty times is what reveals whether your rules are specific enough to be repeated by anyone
other than you.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** Your daily narrative is trending up, your four-hour zone has already been marked below
current price, and price has now traded into that zone. Your one-hour trigger condition has not occurred.
What does the nested approach say to do?

- A. Take the entry now, because two of the three conditions are met.
- B. Ignore the trigger, since lower timeframes are noise.
- C. Wait, because the zone sets location but the trigger still has to time the entry.
- D. Re-mark the zone around current price so that all three conditions line up.

**Correct answer:** C

**Why:** Each layer has one job — the narrative sets direction, the zone sets location and the trigger
times the entry — so an unmet trigger delays the decision rather than cancelling or overriding it.
