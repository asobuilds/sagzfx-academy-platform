# Institutional Order Flow

## 1. What You'll Learn

- What order flow means in plain terms, and why buying and selling always interact with available liquidity.
- The difference between what a candlestick chart records and what it cannot possibly show you.
- How to separate a factual observation from an interpretation in your own written analysis.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

Order flow is the study of how buy and sell orders meet each other in a market. Every price on your chart
is the result of a transaction: someone bought and someone sold, at that moment, in that quantity. When
more aggressive buying meets limited selling interest, price moves up quickly. When a large seller meets
a thin book of buyers, price moves down quickly. That much is mechanical and uncontroversial.

The confusion begins with the word "institutional". Many trading courses use it to mean that a chart
pattern reveals what banks are doing. A candlestick chart cannot do that. It records four numbers per
period — open, high, low and close — plus whatever volume your broker reports, and in retail forex even
the volume figure is a tick count from one venue rather than true market-wide volume. From that data you
cannot identify which participant placed which order, what their intent was, or whether an order came from
a hedge fund, a corporate treasurer or a thousand retail traders on the same side.

What you can legitimately observe is behaviour. You can see that price approached a level, moved beyond
it, and then reacted sharply. You can see that a move was forceful with large candles and little overlap,
or slow and overlapping. You can see where price left gaps between candles. These are facts about price.
They are useful, and they are all the chart honestly gives you.

The professional discipline of this lesson is to keep observation and interpretation in separate columns.
"Price traded 12 pips above the 1.0850 high and closed the hour back below it" is an observation.
"Banks swept the stops above 1.0850 to fill sell orders" is an interpretation. The interpretation may
sometimes be a reasonable working hypothesis, but it is not evidence, and it should never be the reason
a trade is taken.

Why does the distinction matter in practice? Because interpretations feel like knowledge. A trader who
believes they can see institutional intent will hold a losing position far longer than one who knows they
are looking at a pattern and a hypothesis. The first trader thinks the market owes them confirmation. The
second knows they are testing a proposition that can fail.

The practical alternative is to convert every interpretation into a testable condition. Instead of "there
is institutional selling here", write "price reacted twice from this zone with a strong rejection, so I
will treat it as a supply area and require a close below the recent low as confirmation before acting".
That statement uses only observable evidence, states the trigger, and can be shown to be wrong.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to illustrate the process. Suppose it is a Tuesday during the
London session and you are watching EUR/USD on the fifteen-minute chart. The Asian session has left a
narrow range between 1.0842 and 1.0860.

At 09:15, price rises to 1.0868 — eight pips above the Asian high — and within the same fifteen-minute
period closes back at 1.0856. Volume on your platform ticks higher during that period, though you know
that figure only reflects activity on your broker's feed.

Here is how you would record it. Observation: price exceeded the Asian range high by 8 pips and closed the
period back inside the range. Observation: the rejection candle was larger than the previous four.
Interpretation: the move above the high may have been an attempt to trigger orders resting there, and the
failure to hold may suggest sellers are active above 1.0860.

The next step is to say what would confirm or deny it, before anything happens. If your hypothesis is that
sellers are active above the range, a close below 1.0842 — the other side of the range — would support it.
If instead price closes above 1.0868 and holds, the hypothesis is wrong.

Notice what the example does not tell you. It does not say price then fell, and it does not say the level
held. It might have broken straight through 1.0868 and continued. The point of the exercise is the
separation of columns, not the outcome.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Describing a chart pattern as proof of what banks did, when the chart cannot show participant identity.
- Reading broker tick volume as if it were true market-wide volume.
- Confusing a well-argued interpretation with evidence, and then sizing a position as though it were certain.
- Building a whole strategy on the assumed intent of unseen participants rather than on price behaviour.
- Failing to write down what would disprove the interpretation, so it can never be shown to be wrong.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Write down what you can see and what you think separately, and only ever risk money on the first column.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. It uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed, and nothing
you do here can earn or lose you real funds.

Choose one pair and one session. Find five recent occasions where price moved beyond an obvious recent
high or low and then behaved noticeably — either rejecting or continuing.

Draw a two-column table. In the left column, record only what the candles show, using numbers: the level,
the maximum distance beyond it in pips, how the period closed, and how the candle size compared with the
previous few. In the right column, write your interpretation of what that behaviour might indicate. Then,
under each row, add one line stating the price action that would prove your interpretation wrong.

*Why this matters:* practising the two-column split on historical examples builds the habit of noticing
when you have crossed from evidence into storytelling, and adding the disproof line makes each
interpretation testable rather than decorative.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** Which of the following statements about a candlestick chart is accurate?

- A. It shows the identity of the participants behind each order.
- B. It proves when institutional orders are being placed.
- C. It records open, high, low and close for each period, so it shows price behaviour but not participant
  intent.
- D. It shows true market-wide volume for the whole forex market.

**Correct answer:** C

**Why:** A candlestick records four prices per period; it contains no information about who traded or why,
and retail forex volume figures reflect one venue rather than the whole market.
