# Building a Repeatable Trading System

## 1. What You'll Learn

- What the components of a written trading system are, from market universe through to scheduled review.
- Why the test of a system is that another trader could follow your written rules and take the same trades.
- Why repeatability and reviewability are the parts you can actually build, and why no system is guaranteed profitable.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

A trading system is a written set of rules that decides what you look at, when you act, how you manage what
you hold, and how you review what happened. No system is guaranteed profitable, and any material
that tells you otherwise is selling something. What a system can reliably do is make your decisions
consistent and reviewable — the only two things a trader fully controls.

The components are the same regardless of style. The market universe is the list of instruments you are
allowed to trade. The timeframe is
the chart you work from, stated exactly. Setup criteria are the conditions that make a chart worth
attention. The trigger is the specific event that turns attention into action. Stop placement is where the
idea is considered dead. The target and management section says what happens after entry: how the position
is managed and when it is closed. Risk states the money at stake per idea. Review states
when you look back, what you record, and how you change the rules. Eight components, and a system missing
any one of them has a gap that will be filled by mood.

The test of a system is repetition. If you handed your written rules to another trader, along with the same
charts and the same time period, would they take the same trades you took? If the answer is "roughly" or
"they would know what I meant", the rules are not finished. Words like "clean structure", "strong momentum"
and "obvious rejection" feel precise while you are writing them and dissolve the moment someone else tries
to apply them. Replaceable rules read more like: the four-hour closes below the prior swing low, and the stop
sits beyond the high of the trigger candle.

There is a second test, and it is the one that keeps a system honest over time: reviewability. A system
extends that into a routine. You record every trade the
rules allowed, and also the setups you skipped and why. You review on a schedule rather than after a bad
day, change one variable at a time, and write the date and the reason beside the change. A system
that is edited constantly in response to recent results is not being improved; it is being restarted, and
restarts destroy the only evidence you have.

Markets do not owe any set of rules a result, and a
rule set that is followed perfectly can still produce a losing period. The value of a system is that when
that happens, you can open the record and ask a specific question — did I follow the rules, and were the
rules specific enough to follow? — instead of guessing.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to illustrate the process.

Suppose your written system allows four major pairs, and one of them is GBP/JPY, worked from the four-hour
chart during the London session.

The setup criteria say: price must have swept a prior four-hour high by at least fifteen pips during the
previous eight hours. Suppose GBP/JPY swept the high at 188.40 and printed 188.55. Condition met. The
trigger says: a four-hour close back below 188.40. Suppose price closes the four-hour candle at 188.20.
Trigger met. The stop rule says: beyond the extreme of the sweep, so 188.60, which is forty pips from the
trigger close. The risk rule says half a percent of the account, so the size is calculated from that forty
pips. The management rule says what happens if the position moves twenty pips in favour, and where it is
closed if the rules are not satisfied.

Every one of those steps is a do-this-then-check-that line that another trader could reproduce from the
chart without asking you a question.

The setup can fail: the stop can be taken and the rules would still have been followed exactly as written.
A system that includes the possibility of being wrong is a complete system; one that assumes it will not be
is a wish.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Writing rules with words like "strong" or "clean" that cannot be checked by anyone else.
- Adding a new rule after a single loss, making every result unattributable.
- Recording only the trades taken and not the setups skipped, which hides rule drift entirely.
- Reviewing the system on the day the account feels worst, and calling that discipline.
- Treating a system as a promise about outcomes rather than a description of your own decisions.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Write the rules so plainly that a stranger could take the same trades from the same chart, then review the
record on a schedule instead of reacting to the last result.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. It uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed, and nothing you
do there can earn or lose you real funds.

Write your system as one page with eight numbered headings: market universe, timeframe, setup criteria,
trigger, stop placement, target and management, risk, review. Under each heading write rules that can be
checked without interpretation.

Then apply the whole page to fifteen past sessions of one pair without looking ahead, logging every session
as taken or skipped and writing the rule number that decided it. Finally, ask another person to read your
page and mark every line they could not apply. Rewrite those lines until no marks remain.

*Why this matters:* a rule that only you can interpret cannot be reviewed, and review is the only mechanism
that tells you whether your decisions came from your rules or from your mood.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** You show your written system to another trader, and they say they would have taken different
trades on three of the last fifteen setups. What is the most useful conclusion?

- A. The system is broken and should be replaced entirely.
- B. The other trader is wrong, because the system is personal.
- C. Some rules are not specific enough to be applied the same way by two people, so they need rewriting.
- D. The system needs a new indicator added to remove the ambiguity.

**Correct answer:** C

**Why:** Repeatability is the test of a system, so when two people read the same rules and reach different
decisions, the rules are ambiguous and should be rewritten until they can be checked by anyone.
