# Creating a Personal Trading Plan

## 1. What You'll Learn

- Which decisions a trading plan must fix in advance, and why each one has to be settled before the market is open.
- How the sections of a plan fit together, from markets and sessions through to risk limits and the conditions under which you stop.
- Why the plan is a living document that changes only at a scheduled review, never while a trade is running.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

A trading plan is a written document that answers, in advance, every decision you would otherwise make in the
moment. It is not a forecast; it is the set of constraints you choose for yourself, recorded so you can check
them afterwards. The reason it must be written is that the moment of a live trade is a bad time to think. Price is moving, money is at risk, and the part of
you that wants to be right will start negotiating. A written plan removes that negotiation, because the answer
was already decided when you were calm.

By this point in the tier you have produced every raw material the plan needs. The swing definition and
structural reading from the first module of the tier. Entry models, risk-to-reward and trade management from
the Market Structure tier. The execution record and contingency note from the previous module. The plan
assembles them into one document, and the assembly is the work: a plan whose sections contradict each other is
worse than no plan, because it looks authoritative while being unusable.

The sections follow a fixed order, and each one narrows the next. Markets states which instruments you trade
and which you deliberately do not. Sessions states the hours in which you are allowed to act, defined by the
trading centres rather than by when you happen to feel alert. Timeframes states the analysis timeframe and the
decision timeframe, and how they relate. Setups names the structural conditions you are willing to trade, taken
from the entry models you have practised. Entry trigger states the exact condition that turns a setup into an
order. Stop names the level and the reason it is where it is. Target states how you will exit, by fixed level or
by a management rule, using the methods from the Market Structure tier. Risk limits cap how much is committed at
once, how much is risked on a single trade, and how much can be lost across a day or a week before you stop.
Review cadence states when you will sit down and read your own records. And finally, conditions under which you
stop: the states — personal, technical or market-based — in which you do not trade at all.

That last section is the one most people omit. A plan that only describes how to trade is incomplete: you need
to have decided in advance what takes you out of the market — a set number of losses in a defined period, a
platform behaving unreliably, or a day you have not prepared for.

The plan is a living document, but it changes on one condition only: through review, using evidence from your
own records, at a scheduled time, never mid-trade. A rule changed while a position is open is not a plan
evolving; it is you overriding yourself, which is the behaviour the document exists to prevent.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These numbers are hypothetical and illustrate the process only. Suppose you are drafting the setups section of
the plan. You are working on GBP/USD on the four-hour chart with the swing rule you wrote earlier in the tier:
three candles on each side. The condition you are willing to act on is a break of a major swing low followed by
a return to it, a shape your entry model from the Market Structure tier already describes.

You now have to commit numbers. Entry trigger: a four-hour close beyond the swing low at 1.2690, with a limit
order placed at the level on the retest. Stop: 1.2725, above the swing high formed on the return, where your
reading is wrong. Target: a fixed level at 1.2580, giving a risk of 35 pips against a reward of 110. Risk limit:
no more than half of one percent of account equity on this single trade, and no more than three open positions
in the same direction across all pairs. Session: London and New York hours only. Stop conditions: two losses in
a day, or a platform that has dropped connection twice in a session.

Read the numbers back and check they do not contradict each other. If the stop distance makes position size
exceed your risk cap, the setup is not tradable at that size — decided now, in writing, not later.

What the walkthrough establishes is that the plan is internally consistent before it is used. It says nothing
about the outcome. The trade could reach the stop, reach the target, or never trigger; the plan's job is to make
your behaviour identical in all three cases, not to predict which occurs.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Writing a plan that describes strategy but never states the conditions under which you stop trading altogether.
- Setting risk limits as percentages of a round number rather than of actual account equity, so the cap drifts without anyone deciding it should.
- Editing a rule while a position is open, then recording the edit as though it had always been the plan.
- Listing five pairs and three sessions and calling it diversification, when it is really three chances to make the same mistake at once.
- Leaving the trigger vague — "when it looks like it is going to hold" — which returns every decision to the moment it was meant to be taken away from.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Write your plan when the market is closed, and change it only at a scheduled review, never in the middle of an open trade.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. It uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed, and nothing you do
here can earn or lose you real funds.

Produce a one-page written plan with all eleven sections in order: markets, sessions, timeframes, setups, entry
trigger, stop, target, management, risk limits, review cadence, and the conditions under which you stop. The
trigger, stop and target sections must contain a specific level or rule, not an adjective.

Then read the plan as if someone else had written it, and list every place where a decision is still left to the
moment. Rewrite those lines until they are checkable — until another person could tell from the plan whether you
followed it.

Finally, set your review cadence in your calendar and write the date of your first review on the plan itself.

*Why this matters:* a plan is only a constraint if it is already decided, and the sections that are hardest to
write — risk limits and stop conditions — are what stop a bad day from becoming a decision made in frustration.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** A setup in your plan triggers and price moves against you. Your written stop is at 1.2725, but
price is at 1.2740 and your original reading still feels intact, so you move the stop further away. What does
this lesson say about that decision?

- A. It is good management, because the plan is a guide rather than a rule.
- B. It is acceptable as long as you record the change in your journal afterwards.
- C. It is the behaviour the plan exists to prevent, because widening a stop means the stop and the position size your risk limit depends on are no longer fixed.
- D. It is correct, provided the new stop keeps the risk-to-reward ratio above a fixed multiple.

**Correct answer:** C

**Why:** The stop level and the risk cap are set together in advance; moving the stop mid-trade silently increases the money at risk, and is a change that belongs to a scheduled review.
