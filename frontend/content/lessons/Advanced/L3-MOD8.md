# Advanced Order Blocks

## 1. What You'll Learn

- How to identify the last opposing candle before a displacement, and why that specific candle is the one you mark.
- The difference between a refined zone and the full candle range, and what each version costs you.
- What a breaker block is, and how a zone's mitigation status changes whether it is worth using at all.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

In the Market Structure tier you learned what an order block is and why the definition you write down
matters more than the version you choose. This lesson moves to the three decisions that definition leaves
open: which candle is the block, how much of it to mark, and what to do with it once price has finished
with it.

The block is the last opposing candle before the displacement. Opposing means it points against the
direction of the move you are marking: for an upward displacement, the block is a down candle. Last means
the final one, immediately before the move. Displacement means the strong move itself, the leg where price
travels quickly enough to break a swing point and leave little trading behind it. The word "last" does the
heavy lifting. Traders routinely mark a candle two or three positions back because it looks bigger or
lines up with a level they already wanted. The rule is positional, not aesthetic: if an opposing candle
sits between your candidate and the displacement, your candidate is not the block.

The second decision is how much of the candle to mark. A candle has a body — the distance between its open
and close — and a range that includes its upper and lower wicks. A refined zone narrows the block to the
part of the candle that produced the move, usually the body edge nearest the displacement and the gap left
by the move itself, discarding the wick that points the other way. Refinement is a trade, not an upgrade.
A tight zone gives cleaner decisions and more occasions when price never reaches it at all; a wide zone
gives more touches and more cases where you cannot tell whether the zone was respected or simply
traversed.

The third decision concerns what the block has become. If price later returns and reacts from the other
side, some traders call it a breaker block: a zone that failed in one direction and is watched in the
opposite direction on the second visit. Whether you use the breaker idea is a choice you make in writing.
Do not keep a failed zone alive as its original idea and call it a breaker only when it suits you.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to illustrate the process. Suppose it is a London session and
you are studying USD/JPY on the one-hour chart.

Price has been falling. A down candle spans 148.85 to 148.98 on the full range, with a body from 148.90 to
148.95. The candle immediately after it opens at 148.99 and closes at 149.62 on a body far larger than any
of the previous ten, and on the way it takes out the prior swing high at 149.30. Under the positional
rule, the 148.85-to-148.98 candle is the block, because it is the last opposing candle before the
displacement.

Now apply the second decision. The full range gives a zone 13 pips tall from 148.85 to 148.98. The
body-only version gives 5 pips from 148.90 to 148.95. A refined version might give roughly 148.93 to
148.99.

Write down which one you are using and what it costs. With the full range you would need price to trade
into 148.85 to 148.98 before the zone is tested, and a one-hour close below 148.85 makes it invalid. With
the body version you are waiting for 148.90 to 148.95, and 148.90 becomes the invalidation.

Notice what the example does not tell you. It does not say price returned to 148.98, and it does not say
it stayed above 148.90. A block that price never revisits is an ordinary outcome, and a block that price
closes straight through is equally ordinary. The walkthrough fixes the identification and the
invalidation; it does not predict the visit.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Marking a block two or three candles back because it is larger or looks tidier than the true one.
- Switching between the full range and the body version depending on which keeps your idea alive.
- Refining a zone so tightly that price rarely reaches it, then calling every near-miss a test.
- Keeping a zone alive after price closes through it and re-labelling it a breaker when convenient.
- Using a block without stating its mitigation state, so used and fresh zones look identical.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Mark the last opposing candle before the displacement, write down whether you are using the body or the full range, and retire a block the moment price closes through it.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. It uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed, and nothing
you do here can earn or lose you real funds.

Choose one pair and one timeframe. Identify ten displacements that clearly broke a swing point, and for
each mark three candidate zones: the true last opposing candle on its full range, the same candle on its
body only, and your refined version.

For each of the ten, record three things: the boundary in pips of all three versions, the candle number
counted backwards from the displacement to your true block, and the close level that would invalidate each
version. Then sort the ten into those where the three versions sat within a few pips of each other and
those where they did not, and write one sentence explaining the difference in the wide cases.

*Why this matters:* marking three versions of the same block side by side makes the cost of each choice
visible in pips rather than in theory, and counting backwards to the true block breaks the habit of
selecting the candle that looks most convincing.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** On the one-hour chart, a down candle is followed by two smaller down candles and then by a
large bullish candle that breaks a swing high. Which candle is the order block under the positional rule?

- A. The largest of the two smaller down candles, because size determines which candle was active.
- B. The down candle immediately before the bullish displacement, because the rule is positional rather than aesthetic.
- C. The first down candle in the sequence, because it began the decline.
- D. The bullish displacement candle itself, because that is where the buying occurred.

**Correct answer:** B

**Why:** The block is defined by position — the last opposing candle before the displacement — so a
candle further back is not the block no matter how large or tidy it looks.
