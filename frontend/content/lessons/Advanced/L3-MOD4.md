# Liquidity Engineering

## 1. What You'll Learn

- How liquidity pools form from equal highs, equal lows, trendline touches, session extremes and round numbers.
- Why resting orders cluster at predictable prices, and how price interacts with them mechanically.
- How to map the liquidity on a chart before deciding whether any of it matters to your plan.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

Liquidity, in the plain sense this course uses, is the set of orders resting at particular prices that are
available to be traded against. When there is plenty of resting sell interest, a buy order fills easily and
price barely moves. When
resting interest is thin, the same buy order consumes several price levels and price jumps. Liquidity
engineering is the study of where those resting orders collect, why they cluster in predictable places, and
how price tends to move toward and through them. The phrase describes structure; it is not a plan and not a
claim about anyone's intention.

Equal highs and equal lows are the clearest example of a pool. When price reaches the same level twice without exceeding it, anyone watching that level
has a reference point, and the second touch confirms it. Traders place stop-loss orders just beyond it,
because a stop placed past a level that has twice refused to break feels protected. Add the entries of breakout traders who want to trade the level if it finally gives
way, and you have a build-up of orders at one price.

Trendline liquidity works the same way with a moving reference. A line drawn through three rising lows is
watched by many participants, and stops tend to accumulate a little beneath it, just as breakout orders
accumulate above it. Session highs and lows — the extreme of the Asian, London or New York session — create
the same effect, because many participants reference those levels for the same reasons. So do round numbers
such as 1.1000 or 150.00, because they are memorable and easy to place an order at.

The pool is a consequence of many
independent decisions to place orders near the same obvious prices. What price does with the pool is a separate question. Price may approach a cluster of
resting orders, trade through it, and then behave differently than it did before — because once those orders
are consumed, the obstacle that was slowing movement is no longer there. That is the mechanical reason price
often moves faster just after an obvious level breaks: the resting interest has been used up.

The practical use of this lesson is mapping, not predicting. You mark where the pools are before you look for
entries, so that you know which levels are significant and which are incidental. A level with no resting
orders behind it is just a line. A level where stops, breakout orders and round-number interest all sit at
once is a level worth planning around.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to illustrate the process. Suppose it is a Thursday and you are
looking at USD/JPY on the one-hour chart, with the London session underway.

You mark the Asian session range. Its high is 149.62 and its low is 149.18, and the session touched 149.62
twice without closing above it, leaving an equal high. That equal high is your first pool: stops from sellers
who entered near it sit just above 149.62, and breakout buyers have orders waiting there too. Your second
pool is the round number at 150.00, 38 pips away and not yet visited this week. Your third is a rising
trendline drawn through three higher lows at 149.10, 149.24 and 149.35, with stops likely resting a few pips
beneath it.

At 09:40 price dips to 149.31, which is below the trendline but above the third higher low. That is an
interaction with trendline liquidity, though the candle closes back at 149.44 — a wick rather than a close.

What you have is a map: an equal-high pool above, a round number further above, and a trendline pool beneath.
What you do not have is a direction. Price could trade up through 149.62 and accelerate on the way toward
150.00, or it could close decisively below 149.24 and put the rising trendline structure in question. Both
are consistent with the map. The exercise is knowing where the orders are, not guessing which pool gets
visited first.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Drawing every level and treating them all as equal liquidity.
- Assuming a pool will hold, rather than asking what if it is consumed.
- Describing a sweep as "them hunting my stop" instead of where orders sat.
- Marking liquidity after the move, when the level that mattered is already obvious.
- Ignoring the session you are trading, so a London extreme and an Asian extreme look alike.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Map where the orders are likely to sit before you decide what you think, because a level means nothing until
you know what is resting behind it.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. It uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed, and nothing you
do here can earn or lose you real funds.

Choose one major pair and one session. On the fifteen-minute chart, mark the session high and the session low
for the last five sessions. Then mark any equal highs or equal lows, any round numbers within 50 pips of the
current price, and any trendline that price has touched at least three times. Use a different colour for each
category and label every mark with its price.

Below the chart, write one line naming the single level where you judge resting interest to be most
concentrated, and one line saying what price behaviour would tell you that pool has been consumed rather than
respected.

*Why this matters:* mapping the pools before you form a view separates the levels that carry weight from the
ones that merely look tidy, and naming what consumption looks like stops a broken level from being quietly
renamed as a hold.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** Why do equal lows on a chart tend to attract resting orders?

- A. Because a candlestick chart proves which participants placed orders there.
- B. Because many participants independently place stops just below a level that has twice refused to
  break, and breakout orders at the level itself, so their orders cluster at the same price.
- C. Because equal lows are always the exact point where a trend reverses.
- D. Because round numbers only ever appear near equal lows.

**Correct answer:** B

**Why:** The cluster is a mechanical result of many traders using the same obvious price as a reference for
stops and breakout orders; it does not reveal who placed them or guarantee any particular outcome.
