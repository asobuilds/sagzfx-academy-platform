# Fundamental Analysis

## 1. What You'll Learn

- The main drivers worth following: inflation, employment, growth, interest rates and central-bank guidance.
- Why markets react to results relative to expectations and forward guidance, not to whether a number is good or bad.
- How to read an economic calendar and decide in advance whether your approach avoids or trades news.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

Fundamental analysis is the study of the economic forces behind a currency. Where technical analysis looks
at the chart, fundamental analysis looks at the economy the currency represents. The main things worth
following are inflation, employment, growth, interest rates and central-bank guidance — not equally
important at all times, but the categories that move currencies most reliably over months rather than
minutes.

Interest rates deserve special attention because they link the economy to the currency. Higher rates tend
to make a currency more attractive to hold, all else being equal, because holders earn more on deposits
denominated in it. Central banks set policy rates and signal intentions through statements and forecasts,
and that guidance often matters more than the rate itself, because markets try to price the future rather
than the present. A central bank holding rates steady while signalling increases is saying something
different from one holding steady while warning of cuts.

Inflation occupies constant attention for the same reason, since central banks generally respond to
persistent inflation by raising rates. Employment and growth data matter because they indicate how much
room a central bank has to tighten or ease.

The most important idea in this lesson is that markets react to results relative to expectations, not to
whether a number is good or bad in isolation. A strong employment report is not automatically bullish: if
the market expected an even stronger one, a solid number can disappoint and the currency can fall on it.
Conversely, weak data that is less weak than feared can lift a currency. Forecasts are published alongside
the data because the surprise is what moves price, measured against the forecast rather than your own sense
of what a healthy economy looks like.

This applies to guidance as well: traders ask not only what happened but what the data implies about policy
next. A single release rarely changes a trend by itself, and its effect can reverse within hours.

Finally, understand that fundamentals explain direction over long horizons and explain almost nothing about
the next fifteen minutes. The two-way reaction around major releases is why many traders define in advance
whether their approach avoids news or deliberately trades it. Both choices are defensible. Having no rule
at all is not, because it means your exposure to a release is decided by accident.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to illustrate the process. Suppose it is a Thursday and a US
inflation release is scheduled for 13:30 West Africa Time, which is 08:30 in New York. The forecast is 3.2
percent year on year, and the previous reading was 3.4 percent. You are watching EUR/USD on the
fifteen-minute chart.

Before the release, your job is preparation rather than prediction. You note the time, forecast and
previous reading, and you check the timezone carefully — an event you thought was at 14:30 is not the event
you planned around. Spreads typically widen around this release, and slippage is possible on stops during
the first seconds, so a stop cannot be relied upon to fill at exactly the price you set.

Now suppose the release comes in at 3.5 percent, above the 3.2 percent forecast. On the surface, higher
inflation is a stronger case for tighter policy, which might support the dollar. But the reaction is not
determined by that reasoning alone. Price might have moved sharply lower on EUR/USD, and it might equally
have spiked in both directions within minutes as the market weighed the report's details and what they
implied for policy.

What the example does establish is the value of a written rule. If your plan says you do not hold positions
through major releases, the two-way volatility would have been irrelevant to you. If it says you trade them
deliberately, you would have defined your maximum risk and accepted possible slippage. Either way, the
decision was made before the number arrived.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Reading a data release as simply good or bad, instead of comparing it with the published forecast.
- Ignoring the timezone on an economic calendar and mistaking the event's actual time.
- Holding a position through a major release with no plan, because the trade "was going well".
- Assuming a stop-loss will fill at its exact price during a volatile release, when slippage is possible.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Decide before the session whether your approach avoids news or trades it, and never let a release decide for
you.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** US inflation is forecast at 3.2 percent and comes in at 3.5 percent. Which statement best
describes how the market is likely to respond?

- A. The currency will rise, because higher inflation always strengthens a currency.
- B. The currency will fall, because higher inflation is bad for an economy.
- C. The reaction depends on how the figure compares with expectations and what it implies for future
  policy, and it can move in either direction.
- D. There will be no reaction, because forecasts already price in all data.

**Correct answer:** C

**Why:** Markets react to the surprise relative to the forecast and to what the data suggests about future
policy, which is why a release can produce movement in either direction.
