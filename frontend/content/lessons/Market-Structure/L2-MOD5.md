# Liquidity & liquidity sweeps

## 1. What You'll Learn

- What liquidity means in a market, and why obvious highs and lows attract attention.
- What a liquidity sweep is, and how to describe one without claiming to know who caused it.
- Why a sweep is a location to watch, not an entry signal on its own.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

Liquidity is the ability to transact without moving the price too much. A market is liquid when a large
order can be filled without shifting the quote noticeably, and illiquid when even a modest order pushes
price around. This matters to you for two reasons: liquid markets have tighter spreads, which lowers your
cost of trading, and liquidity is not spread evenly across the price range — it clusters where orders are
known to sit.

Where do orders cluster? At levels that everyone can see: a clearly marked swing high, a swing low that
has held three times, an equal-highs pattern, or the obvious edge of a range. Buy stops sit above obvious
highs, because that is where short traders place protective stops, and where breakout traders place entry
orders. Sell stops sit below obvious lows for the mirror-image reason. These are not secret levels; they
are the levels any attentive person can mark.

A liquidity sweep, sometimes called a stop run or a liquidity grab, is what happens when price moves
beyond one of these watched levels and then reacts: it trades through the level, takes out whatever orders
were resting there, and returns to the range. That is the observable pattern — a move beyond a watched
level, followed by a reaction back.

Here is the part that matters most, and it is where a lot of teaching on this topic goes wrong. Not every
sweep reverses. Some moves beyond an obvious level are simply breakouts that keep going, and there is no
way to tell the difference at the moment the level breaks. A chart shows you that price moved beyond a
level and what happened afterwards. It does not show you who placed which order, or why. Describing a
sweep as "institutions collecting liquidity" is a story layered on top of the observation, and it is not
something the chart can prove.

So treat a sweep as a location and a sequence of events, not as a signal. The useful question is not
"did smart money sweep this level," but "what evidence would tell me the move failed?" That evidence might
be a candle closing back inside the range, a failure to make a second push, or a structure break in the
opposite direction. Decide in advance which you require.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to illustrate the pattern. Suppose you are watching EUR/USD on
the fifteen-minute chart during a London session. Price has spent the morning ranging between 1.0800 and
1.0860. It has touched 1.0860 three separate times without closing above it, so you mark that as an
obvious high where stops and breakout orders likely sit.

At 09:45, a candle trades up to 1.0866, wicking beyond the level, and closes back at 1.0854 — inside the
range. On the candle after, price closes at 1.0841. The observed sequence is: a move beyond a watched
level, followed by a return inside the range.

Now state the invalidation before you consider anything further. If your reading is that the push above
1.0860 failed, then a subsequent candle closing back above 1.0866 would weaken that reading, and a close
above 1.0870 would suggest the move beyond the level was a genuine breakout rather than a failed push. You
might also require a structure break, such as a close below the session low at 1.0812, before treating the
sequence as meaningful rather than merely interesting.

What this example does not do is tell you what happened next. Price might have continued lower and
resolved the range to the downside. It might equally have reversed back up, closed above 1.0870, and
continued higher, leaving the sweep looking, in hindsight, like a routine pullback inside a range about to
break upward. Not every sweep reverses, and the label alone does not tell you which kind you face.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Treating every move beyond a level as a sweep, when some are breakouts that continue.
- Claiming the chart shows who placed orders or why, when it only shows price behaviour.
- Entering immediately at the level break with no confirmation and no stated invalidation.
- Marking so many levels as "liquidity" that the label stops discriminating between them.
- Assuming a sweep must reverse because a previous sweep reversed.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Mark the level, describe the move beyond it and the reaction that followed, and never claim the chart told
you who was trading.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. It uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed, and nothing
you do here can earn or lose you real funds.

On three pairs, mark every obvious level you can identify on a single timeframe: clear swing highs, clear
swing lows, equal highs and equal lows, and range edges. Keep the total to five or fewer levels per chart
so the exercise stays meaningful.

For each level, record four things in neutral language: the level, the date and time price moved beyond
it, what price did on the following three candles, and whether your chosen confirmation appeared. Use only
observable descriptions — "price closed back inside the range" — and avoid any sentence about who was
buying or selling.

Finish by tallying how many of the levels produced a return inside the range and how many continued
through. Report the counts without judging them.

*Why this matters:* seeing with your own tally that sweeps reverse sometimes and not others is the most
reliable cure for treating the label as a signal, and describing behaviour without motives keeps your
analysis honest.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** Price wicks above an obvious swing high at 1.0860, reaching 1.0866, then closes back at
1.0854 inside the range. What can you accurately conclude?

- A. Institutions collected liquidity above the level, so price will now fall.
- B. Price moved beyond a watched level and returned inside the range; whether it reverses is still unknown.
- C. The level was a valid breakout, so the range has resolved upward.
- D. The move was a sweep, so no invalidation level is needed.

**Correct answer:** B

**Why:** The chart shows a move beyond a watched level followed by a return inside the range — it cannot
show who traded or why, and not every sweep reverses.
