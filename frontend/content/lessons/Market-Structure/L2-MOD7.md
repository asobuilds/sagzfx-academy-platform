# Order Blocks

## 1. What You'll Learn

- What an order block is in common smart money concept methods, and how the academy defines one.
- How to test candidate zones against consistent written criteria instead of picking by eye.
- Why a chart cannot reveal who placed orders, and why every block needs an invalidation level.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

In many smart money concept methods, an order block is described as the candle or base that comes
immediately before a strong move which breaks structure. The story told about it is that the last candle
in the opposite direction before a sharp move represents the place where a large participant was active.
A bullish order block is therefore the last down candle before a strong upward move that breaks a swing
high. A bearish order block is the last up candle before a strong downward move that breaks a swing low.

You should notice immediately that definitions vary between teachers. Some use the candle body, some the
full range including wicks, some require the block to sit at a specific structural point, and some require
a fair value gap to be present as well. There is no single standard. This is precisely why the academy
asks you to define an order block in writing and then apply that definition without modification. The value
of the concept comes from consistency, not from the particular version you choose.

A workable definition has four parts. First, the base candle: which candle you treat as the block. Second,
the displacement: what counts as a strong move, usually measured by candle body size or by the fact that
structure broke. Third, the boundaries: body, full range, or a wider area. Fourth, the invalidation: the
price at which you accept the block has failed.

The claim you must not make is that the chart proves who placed orders. A candlestick chart records prices
and volume. It does not record identities, intentions, or order sizes, and no amount of pattern
recognition turns it into a record of who was trading. When this course describes an order block, it is
describing a shape and a location, and asking you to test whether price has historically behaved
interestingly there. That is a testable question. "Banks placed orders here" is not.

So use order blocks as you use supply and demand zones: as marked locations that give you a place to look
and a level at which to admit you were wrong. Blocks are run through regularly, and a block that fails
tells you the same thing a zone that fails tells you — that the reaction you expected did not appear.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to show the mechanics. Suppose you are studying EUR/USD on the
one-hour chart during a London session. Price has been declining. A down candle runs from 1.0760 down to
1.0742. The next candle opens at 1.0743 and closes at 1.0790, a large bullish body, and in doing so it
takes out the prior swing high at 1.0782.

Under the academy definition, the down candle spanning 1.0742 to 1.0760 is a bullish order block, because
it is the last candle in the opposite direction before a strong move that broke structure. You mark the
zone, and you use the full range including the wicks, which is the version you wrote down.

Now apply the criteria rather than your instincts. Displacement: the bullish candle was clearly larger
than recent candles and it broke the 1.0782 swing high, so it qualifies. Boundaries: 1.0742 to 1.0760, a
band eighteen pips wide. Invalidation: a one-hour close below 1.0742 would mean price had traded through
the block entirely, and you would stop treating it as a bullish block.

Then compare it with a second candidate on the same chart — an earlier down candle at 1.0710 to 1.0722
that was followed by a modest bounce which did not break any swing high. Against your criteria, this
second candidate fails the displacement test. What the example does not tell you is whether price ever
returns to 1.0742, or whether it holds if it does. It might react cleanly, and it might slice straight
through to 1.0730 without pausing.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Changing which candle counts as the block depending on which one makes the current setup look better.
- Selecting blocks by eye and then inventing criteria afterwards to justify the choice.
- Claiming the chart shows banks or institutions placing orders at the block.
- Marking blocks with no invalidation, so there is no defined point at which the idea is wrong.
- Treating a block that has already been tested several times as though it were fresh.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Define the block in writing before you look for one, because a definition chosen after the fact describes
your preference rather than the chart.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. It uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed, and nothing
you do here can earn or lose you real funds.

Write your order block definition as four numbered parts: the base candle, what counts as displacement,
the boundaries you use, and the invalidation level. Keep each part to one sentence.

Then find two candidate zones on the same chart — a four-hour chart works well — and rate both against
your written criteria. For each candidate, record whether it passes or fails each of the four parts, and
state which one you would rank higher and why. Include at least one candidate that fails, so the exercise
tests discrimination rather than confirmation.

Finish by writing two sentences on one block that price traded straight through without reacting,
describing only what price did.

*Why this matters:* scoring candidates against a fixed rubric is the skill that separates a repeatable
method from pattern-shopping, and it is the same discipline you will need when ranking setups later in the
course.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** A down candle spans 1.0742 to 1.0760 on the one-hour chart, followed by a large bullish
candle that closes at 1.0790 and breaks the prior swing high at 1.0782. Under the academy definition,
what is this?

- A. Proof that institutions placed buy orders between 1.0742 and 1.0760.
- B. A candidate bullish order block, subject to your written criteria and a stated invalidation level.
- C. An invalid block, because only the candle body may be used as the boundary.
- D. A bearish order block, because the base candle closed lower than it opened.

**Correct answer:** B

**Why:** It fits the shape — the last opposite-direction candle before a strong move that broke structure —
but definitions vary, the chart cannot reveal who traded, and the block still needs an invalidation level.
