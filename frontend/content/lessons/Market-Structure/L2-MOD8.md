# Premium & Discount

## 1. What You'll Learn

- How to choose a meaningful swing range and calculate its midpoint, called equilibrium.
- Why the upper half is called premium and the lower half discount, and what those words actually mean.
- Why these labels are relative to the range you chose, not proof that price is objectively expensive or cheap.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

Premium and discount are terms used in many smart money concept methods to describe where price sits within
a chosen range. The idea comes from ordinary retail logic: you would rather buy something at a discount
than at a premium. Applied to a chart, the argument is that buying in the lower half of a range, or selling
in the upper half, gives a better starting position than doing the opposite.

The method has three steps. First, choose a meaningful swing range. This means identifying a swing low and
a swing high that represent a genuine directional move, not two arbitrary points you selected because they
produced the answer you wanted. Second, mark the midpoint of that range, which is called equilibrium. You
calculate it by adding the high and the low and dividing by two. Third, divide the range: everything above
equilibrium is the premium half, and everything below it is the discount half.

That is the whole mechanic. It is arithmetic, and it is not complicated. What requires care is the first
step, because the choice of range determines everything that follows. If you pick a swing low and swing
high that span the last two weeks, your equilibrium will sit in a very different place from a range drawn
across the last two hours. Neither choice is inherently wrong, but they answer different questions, and you
should be able to say why you chose the range you did before you look at where price is sitting inside it.

The warning for this lesson is about the words themselves. Premium and discount sound like objective
statements about value, as though price above equilibrium is genuinely expensive and price below it is
genuinely cheap. They are not. The labels are entirely relative to the range you drew. Price in the premium
half of a two-hour range may still be in the discount half of a two-week range. Nothing about the midpoint
tells you whether a currency is strong or weak in any real economic sense.

Treat premium and discount as a way of organising a chart into a location map. They tell you where price
sits relative to a reference you chose. They do not tell you what price will do there, and the reaction at
equilibrium is no more guaranteed than the reaction at any other level.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to demonstrate the calculation. Suppose you are looking at
GBP/USD on the four-hour chart during a New York session. You identify a swing low at 1.2450 and a swing
high at 1.2850, a four-hundred-pip range that represents a clear directional leg over the past two weeks.
You choose this range because the leg was clean and both ends are unambiguous swing points.

Equilibrium is the midpoint: 1.2450 plus 1.2850 is 2.5300, divided by two is 1.2650. Everything from 1.2650
up to 1.2850 is the premium half. Everything from 1.2450 up to 1.2650 is the discount half.

Now suppose price is trading at 1.2720. That places it in the premium half, roughly seventy pips above
equilibrium. Under the method, that is the half where a trader looking to buy would exercise more caution
and a trader looking to sell would find the location more interesting. What it is not is a signal. Price at
1.2720 tells you where you are inside your chosen range, and nothing else.

The example deliberately stops there. Price might have continued up through the 1.2850 high, extending the
range and shifting equilibrium higher. It might equally have turned down and travelled all the way through
the discount half to test 1.2450. Both are ordinary, and neither is predicted by the fact that price sat
above the midpoint. If you later redraw the range because price broke out of it, that is normal
maintenance of the reference frame, not the method failing.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Choosing the swing range after seeing where price is, so the label always agrees with the intended trade.
- Treating premium and discount as objective statements about a currency's value.
- Forgetting to redraw the range after price breaks clearly beyond it.
- Using a range so wide or so narrow that the midpoint carries no practical meaning.
- Assuming equilibrium is a level where price must react, rather than a reference point.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Premium and discount describe where price sits inside a range you chose, so state the range before you
state the label.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. It uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed, and nothing
you do here can earn or lose you real funds.

On two pairs, identify a meaningful swing low and swing high on the four-hour chart and calculate
equilibrium by hand, showing the arithmetic. Record the swing low, the swing high, the range in pips,
equilibrium, and where current price sits — premium or discount — with the distance from equilibrium in
pips.

Then repeat the exercise on the fifteen-minute chart for the same two pairs, using the most recent clear
leg. Compare the two sets of labels. Write two or three sentences describing any case where the same price
falls in different halves depending on the range.

*Why this matters:* the comparison between timeframes is what makes the relativity of premium and discount
concrete, and it prevents you from treating the midpoint as a fact about value rather than a reference you
selected.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** You draw a range from a swing low at 1.2450 to a swing high at 1.2850. Price is trading at
1.2720. What is the correct description?

- A. Price is objectively expensive, so it must fall towards equilibrium.
- B. Price is in the premium half, which is a location description relative to your chosen range.
- C. Price is in the discount half, because it is below the swing high.
- D. Equilibrium is at 1.2700, so price is just above it.

**Correct answer:** B

**Why:** Equilibrium is 1.2650, so 1.2720 sits in the upper or premium half — a statement about position
within a range you selected, not about value or direction.
