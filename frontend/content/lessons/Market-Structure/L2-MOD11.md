# Risk-to-Reward

## 1. What You'll Learn

- How to calculate risk-to-reward from planned loss and planned gain, expressed in pips or in R.
- Why a favourable ratio does not guarantee profitability, and how win rate and costs change the picture.
- How to compare ratios across hypothetical setups without assuming the reward will be reached.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

Risk-to-reward compares the amount you plan to lose if a trade fails with the amount you plan to gain if it
succeeds. If your stop-loss sits twenty pips from your entry and your target sits forty pips away, the
planned ratio is 1:2, usually written as 2R. You are risking one unit to pursue two. Traders often express
this in a shorthand called R, where 1R is your risk on the trade, so a 2R target means a profit twice the
size of your risk.

The calculation itself is simple, but two details are easy to get wrong. First, the ratio must be based on
the prices you actually plan to use. If your stop sits where the setup is invalidated, that distance is
your risk, full stop. Moving it tighter to improve the ratio moves it away from the invalidation level,
which weakens the trade. Second, spread and commission are real costs: a twenty-pip risk with a two-pip
spread is effectively twenty-two pips before the market moves at all.

The most important thing to understand about risk-to-reward is what it does not do. A favourable ratio does
not make a trade likely to succeed. It tells you the terms you are accepting, not the probability of
collecting them. A model that risks twenty pips to make forty and is right only one time in five loses
money steadily, because the losses arrive four times as often as the wins. Conversely, a model risking
twenty pips to make twenty can be profitable if it wins more often than it loses. The ratio and the win
rate work together, and neither one alone tells you whether a method makes sense.

This is why experienced traders talk about expectancy rather than reward alone. Expectancy combines your
win rate with your average win and average loss, and it answers the question that actually matters: across
many trades, does this method produce more than it costs? A high ratio with a low win rate and a low ratio
with a high win rate can both work. What cannot work is a ratio chosen to look impressive while the actual
outcomes are ignored.

One final caution about targets. A target is a plan for where you intend to exit, not a promise that price
will reach it. Price frequently travels part of the way and returns, and part of the way is still a loss.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to demonstrate the arithmetic. Suppose you are considering
EUR/USD on the one-hour chart during a London session. Your entry is at 1.0900, based on a fifteen-minute
trigger. Your stop sits at 1.0880, below the level that would invalidate the setup, so your risk is twenty
pips. Your target is the prior swing high at 1.0940, forty pips away. The planned ratio is 1:2.

Now include costs. If the spread on EUR/USD is 1.2 pips, your effective risk is 21.2 pips and your reward
net of the spread is roughly 38.8 pips, so the real ratio is closer to 1:1.83 rather than a clean 1:2. That
difference matters over hundreds of trades, which is why the calculation is worth doing properly rather
than approximating.

Next, apply the same arithmetic to a second hypothetical setup on the same pair. Entry at 1.0860, stop at
1.0850, target at 1.0875. Risk is ten pips, reward is fifteen, so the ratio is 1:1.5. Under the numbers
alone, the first setup looks better. But the first setup needs price to travel forty pips against a stop
twenty pips away, while the second needs fifteen pips against a stop ten pips away. Which is more likely to
reach its target is a question the ratio does not answer, and neither setup is guaranteed to reach
anything at all. Both may fail, and the one with the better ratio may fail more often.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Tightening the stop to improve the ratio, moving it away from the level that actually invalidates the idea.
- Forgetting spread and commission, so the real ratio is worse than the one on paper.
- Assuming a 1:3 setup is profitable without any idea of how often it reaches the target.
- Choosing a distant target purely because it makes the ratio look attractive.
- Moving the target closer mid-trade out of fear while leaving the original stop in place.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Set your stop where the idea is invalidated and let the ratio follow, never the other way round.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. It uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed, and nothing
you do here can earn or lose you real funds.

Calculate the planned ratio for three hypothetical setups of your own, using one pair and one timeframe.
For each, record the entry, the stop, the target, the risk in pips, the reward in pips, and the ratio. Note
the current spread for the pair and recalculate each ratio with the spread included.

Then, for each of the three, write one sentence stating what would have to be true for the trade to reach
its target — for example, "price would need to travel back through the zone it just left". Finally, look at
each setup on the chart and record whether price actually reached the target, the stop, or neither.

*Why this matters:* calculating the ratio with costs included, and then checking what price actually did,
turns risk-to-reward from a number you quote into a measurement you can learn from.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** You enter at 1.0900 with a stop at 1.0880 and a target at 1.0940. What does the 1:2 ratio
tell you?

- A. The trade is twice as likely to win as to lose.
- B. You are risking twenty pips to pursue forty, before costs — it says nothing about the probability of
  reaching the target.
- C. The trade will be profitable if you take enough of them.
- D. The maximum loss is forty pips if the stop is not honoured.

**Correct answer:** B

**Why:** Risk-to-reward describes the terms you are accepting, not the odds — win rate and costs determine
whether those terms produce a profit over a sample.
