# Fair Value Gaps (FVG)

## 1. What You'll Learn

- What a fair value gap is, and how to identify one using a three-candle pattern.
- How to mark the boundaries of a bullish and a bearish gap on a chart.
- Why a fair value gap is an imbalance area, not proof of institutional orders, and why it may never fill.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

A fair value gap, commonly shortened to FVG, is a chart pattern defined by three consecutive candles. In
the terminology used by many smart money concept traders, a bullish fair value gap is the space between
the high of candle one and the low of candle three, when candle two in the middle moves upward with enough
force that the first and third candles do not overlap. A bearish fair value gap is the mirror image: the
space between the low of candle one and the high of candle three, when the middle candle moves down
forcefully.

The reason the pattern is noticed at all is that it marks a place where price moved quickly, with little
trading in between. When price travels fast, fewer transactions occur at the prices it passes through. The
area where that happened is what traders call an imbalance. Some traders expect price to return to that
area later and "fill" it, because the market is thought to prefer trading where both buyers and sellers
were active.

Marking a gap is mechanical once you have the rule. Find three candles where the middle one shows a strong
directional move. For a bullish gap, take the high of the first candle as the bottom of your zone and the
low of the third candle as the top, and draw the rectangle between them. For a bearish gap, take the low of
the first candle and the high of the third. If the first and third candles overlap, there is no gap by this
definition, and you move on.

Two warnings belong in this lesson from the start. The first is that a fair value gap is a chart-defined
area and nothing more. It does not prove that any particular participant placed orders, and it does not
reveal intent. Describing a gap as "where institutions left orders" is an interpretation, not an
observation, and it is not something a candlestick chart can demonstrate.

The second warning is that gaps may or may not fill. Some are revisited within minutes and price reacts
visibly. Others are never returned to at all, and price continues away from them. A gap that does not fill
is not a broken rule or a market malfunction. It simply means the expected return did not happen, which is
an ordinary outcome. Treat a gap as a location worth marking and watching, and treat a fill as one possible
development rather than a scheduled event.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to demonstrate the construction. Suppose you are looking at
GBP/USD on the fifteen-minute chart during a New York session. You find three consecutive candles: candle
one has a high of 1.2680, candle two opens near 1.2682 and closes near 1.2735 with a large body, and candle
three has a low of 1.2700.

Under the bullish definition, the gap is the space between the high of candle one at 1.2680 and the low of
candle three at 1.2700. You draw your zone between those two prices, a band twenty pips wide. Nothing about
the move is in dispute: candle two did travel quickly and the outer candles did not overlap.

Now define what would matter. If you were inclined to watch this zone for a reaction, the invalidation
would be a close below 1.2680, which would mean price had traded through the entire gap rather than
reacting within it. That level is worth writing down before any further price action appears.

What this example does not tell you is whether price ever returns to 1.2680 to 1.2700, or what happens if
it does. Price might have pulled straight back into the zone within the hour and reacted. It might equally
have continued upward for days without ever trading into that band again, leaving the gap unfilled
indefinitely. Both happen, and the pattern alone does not distinguish between them.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Drawing gaps on every three-candle sequence, including ones where the outer candles clearly overlap.
- Filling the chart with gaps until no single one is meaningful enough to act on.
- Assuming a gap must fill, then holding a position that depends on the return happening.
- Describing a gap as proof that institutions left orders, which the chart cannot show.
- Marking a gap on one timeframe and trading it on another without checking either.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

A fair value gap is an area where price moved quickly, not a promise that price will come back to it.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** Three candles print on the fifteen-minute chart. Candle one has a high of 1.2680. Candle two
closes higher with a large body. Candle three has a low of 1.2700. How do you mark the bullish fair value
gap?

- A. Between 1.2680 and 1.2735, using candle two's close as the upper boundary.
- B. Between the high of candle one at 1.2680 and the low of candle three at 1.2700.
- C. Between the low of candle one and the high of candle three.
- D. There is no gap, because three candles are not enough to define one.

**Correct answer:** B

**Why:** A bullish fair value gap is the space between the high of the first candle and the low of the
third, which is why the middle candle's close is not part of the boundary.
