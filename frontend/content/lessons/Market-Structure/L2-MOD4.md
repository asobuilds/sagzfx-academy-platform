# Supply & Demand

## 1. What You'll Learn

- What supply and demand zones are, and how to mark one on a chart.
- The four things that shape the quality of a zone: departure strength, freshness, context and invalidation.
- Why a zone is a hypothesis about a place to look, not a guaranteed turning point.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

A supply zone is an area on the chart where strong selling previously emerged. A demand zone is an area
where strong buying previously emerged. The reasoning behind them is straightforward: if a large amount
of selling appeared at a particular price, and that price is reached again, the same kind of activity
might appear again. Supply zones sit above current price and are watched for a downward reaction. Demand
zones sit below current price and are watched for an upward reaction.

Marking a zone starts with a sharp move. Find a place where price left an area quickly and decisively,
then mark the base that price left behind — the last consolidation or the candle from which the departure
began. Draw the zone as a rectangle covering that area, not as a single precise line. Zones are areas
because the exact price at which large orders were placed is not visible on a chart.

Four things affect whether a zone deserves your attention. The first is departure strength: a zone from
which price moved away violently carries more weight than one followed by a slow drift. The second is
freshness: a zone that price has not returned to since it formed is generally more interesting than one
that has already been tested three times, because each test consumes some of whatever was there. The
third is context: a demand zone in a market that is clearly trending up means something different from the
same shape in a downtrend, and a zone that lines up with a higher-timeframe level deserves more respect
than one floating in the middle of nowhere. The fourth is invalidation, and it is the one beginners skip.
Every zone needs a stated price at which you would accept the zone has failed — usually a close beyond the
far side of it.

The honest framing is that a zone is a hypothesis. It says: "if price returns here, this is a place worth
watching for a reaction." It does not say price will turn. Zones get run through all the time, and a
market that trades straight through a demand zone without pausing has told you something useful — that
buyers were not there. That is information, not a malfunction.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to show the method. Suppose you are studying GBP/USD on the
four-hour chart during a London session. Price has been drifting sideways around 1.2650 for several hours.
Then, over two candles, it drops from 1.2640 to 1.2510 with large bodies and little overlap. The base it
left behind — the consolidation between 1.2640 and 1.2660 — is your supply zone.

You record it with four notes. Departure strength: strong, two large candles with little overlap. Freshness:
untested, price has not returned since. Context: the zone sits near a level that also appeared on the daily
chart, which adds weight. Invalidation: a four-hour close above 1.2660 would mean price had traded through
the zone rather than reacting to it, and you would stop treating it as supply.

Now price begins to climb back towards the zone. It reaches 1.2600 and continues. This is the point where
discipline matters, because nothing has happened yet that confirms the zone matters. Price might react at
1.2640 and turn down, and it might slice straight through to 1.2700 without slowing. Both are ordinary
outcomes, and the second is common enough that treating a zone as a place to enter without any further
evidence is not a defensible approach. What the zone gives you is a location to watch and a clear level at
which you would admit the idea failed. It gives you nothing more than that.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Marking zones everywhere until the chart is covered in rectangles and none of them mean anything.
- Entering at the first touch of a zone with no confirmation, treating the zone itself as the trigger.
- Ignoring freshness, then wondering why a zone that has been tested four times no longer reacts.
- Skipping the invalidation level, so there is no defined point at which the idea is wrong.
- Assuming a chart can show which institutions placed orders at a zone, when it cannot.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Every zone you mark must come with the price that invalidates it, or it is decoration rather than analysis.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. It uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed, and nothing
you do here can earn or lose you real funds.

Select three pairs and mark one supply zone and one demand zone on each, using a single timeframe such as
the four-hour. For every zone, write four short notes: the strength of the departure, whether the zone is
fresh or already tested, the higher-timeframe context around it, and the exact price that would invalidate
it.

Then find one historical zone on any chart that price traded straight through without reacting, and write
two sentences describing it in neutral language. Do not describe it as a failure of the method; describe
what price actually did.

*Why this matters:* a zone with a written invalidation is the raw material for a stop-loss level later in
the course, and studying a zone that did not hold is what stops you from treating zones as guarantees.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** You mark a demand zone between 1.2510 and 1.2530 on the four-hour chart, and price has
already returned to it twice since it formed. What is the most accurate reading?

- A. The zone is stronger, because it has been respected twice.
- B. The zone is invalid, because zones may only be tested once.
- C. The zone is less fresh, and each test may consume some of the activity that created it.
- D. The zone guarantees a reaction on the third test.

**Correct answer:** C

**Why:** Freshness is one of the four factors that shape a zone's quality, and repeated tests tend to
reduce rather than increase how much attention a zone deserves.
