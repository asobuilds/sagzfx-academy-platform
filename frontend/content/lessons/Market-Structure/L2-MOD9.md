# Multi-Timeframe Analysis

## 1. What You'll Learn

- How to give each timeframe a specific job: context, setup area, and trigger.
- How to write a three-timeframe plan before the session begins rather than during it.
- Why conflicting timeframes are a reason to wait rather than a reason to force a trade.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

Multi-timeframe analysis means looking at the same instrument on more than one timeframe and giving each
one a defined job. The reason for doing this is that a single chart cannot answer every question. A daily
chart shows you the broad context but hides the detail of how price is moving right now. A five-minute
chart shows you that detail but gives you no sense of whether the move matters. Using both, with each
assigned a specific role, produces a more complete picture than either alone.

The structure that works well in practice uses three timeframes, with roughly a factor of four to six
between them. The higher timeframe provides context: what is the broader trend, where are the major levels,
and what is the overall direction of travel. The middle timeframe identifies the setup area: the zone,
level, or region where you would be interested in doing something. The lower timeframe provides the
trigger: the specific, defined event that would tell you the setup is actually developing.

The critical discipline is deciding each timeframe's job in advance, because most confusion in
multi-timeframe analysis comes from not knowing which chart has authority. Without a written answer, you
will resolve that conflict emotionally, and in whatever direction supports the trade you already want.
Write the answer down before the session: the higher timeframe decides direction, the middle timeframe
decides location, and the lower timeframe decides timing. Nothing else gets a vote.

The second discipline concerns conflict. When timeframes disagree, the correct response is usually to wait.
A conflict is information: it tells you the market is in a state your plan does not describe well. Forcing
a trade through a conflict means you have decided to override your own framework, which is exactly the
behaviour a written plan exists to prevent. Waiting costs you nothing but time. There will be other
setups, and they will be easier to read than this one.

One caution: adding timeframes does not add edge. Four or five charts do not make an analysis better than
three; they make it slower and more likely to contain a contradiction you will resolve by preference.
Choose your three, define their jobs, and stop.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to demonstrate the framework. Suppose it is a Tuesday morning
and you are preparing to watch EUR/USD during the London session. On the daily chart, price has been
climbing with higher highs and higher lows, and the most recent swing low is 1.0780. Your context is
bullish, and 1.0780 is the level that would invalidate that context on the daily.

On the four-hour chart, which is your setup timeframe, price has pulled back and is approaching a demand
zone between 1.0840 and 1.0860 that also lines up with a level you marked on the daily. That zone is your
setup area — the region where you would be interested in seeing something happen.

On the fifteen-minute chart, your trigger timeframe, you define the trigger in advance: a candle closing
back above 1.0865, followed by a higher low on that chart. That is a specific, checkable event. If it
occurs, the setup is developing as your plan describes. If it does not occur, nothing happens, and that is
a complete and acceptable outcome for the session.

Now notice what the plan does not contain. It does not say that price will react at the zone, and it does
not say that the bullish daily context guarantees anything. The zone might be traded straight through, and
the daily swing low at 1.0780 might eventually break, which would change your context entirely. A
three-timeframe plan organises your attention. It does not predict. If the timeframes conflict — say the
four-hour chart is clearly making lower highs while the daily remains bullish — your written rule tells you
to wait, and waiting is the correct action rather than a missed opportunity.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Using so many timeframes that contradictions become impossible to resolve.
- Deciding which timeframe wins after the conflict appears, based on the trade you prefer.
- Letting the trigger timeframe quietly override the context timeframe when the trade looks attractive.
- Defining the trigger as a feeling such as "looks strong" rather than a specific, checkable event.
- Forcing a trade through a timeframe conflict instead of waiting for a cleaner setup.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

Give each timeframe one job and write it down, because a timeframe with no assigned job will always be
used to justify the trade you already want.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. It uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed, and nothing
you do here can earn or lose you real funds.

Choose one pair and three timeframes — for example daily, four-hour and fifteen-minute. Write a plan with
four lines: the context from the higher timeframe, the setup area from the middle timeframe, the trigger
from the lower timeframe, and the conditions under which you would not trade at all.

Then watch that pair for one full session without placing any order, and record whether each of the four
lines occurred. If your timeframes conflicted at any point, write down what your plan said to do and
whether you followed it. Finish by stating the one invalidation level that would change your context.

*Why this matters:* recording the no-trade conditions alongside the setup is what turns a chart idea into
a rule you can follow under pressure, and it tests whether your trigger was specific enough to be checked.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** Your daily chart shows a bullish trend, your four-hour chart shows clear lower highs, and your
written plan says the higher timeframe decides direction. What should you do?

- A. Buy, because the higher timeframe always has final authority on entries.
- B. Sell, because the four-hour chart is the most recent information.
- C. Wait, because the timeframes conflict and your plan does not describe this state well.
- D. Switch to a different timeframe until the two agree.

**Correct answer:** C

**Why:** Conflict between timeframes is information that the market is in a state your plan does not cover,
and waiting is the disciplined response rather than forcing a trade.
