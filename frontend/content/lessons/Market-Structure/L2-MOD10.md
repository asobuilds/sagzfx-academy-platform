# Entry Models

## 1. What You'll Learn

- What an entry model is: a repeatable combination of location, trigger and invalidation.
- How to write setup, trigger and no-trade rules that someone else could check on your chart.
- Why a written checklist beats judgement in the moment, and how to test a model on past charts.

## 2. Plain-English Explanation

An entry model is a repeatable combination of three things: a location, a trigger, and an invalidation
level. The location is where you are willing to act — a zone, a level, or an area you have marked in
advance. The trigger is the specific event that tells you the setup is developing at that location. The
invalidation is the price at which you accept the idea is wrong. When all three are written down and
applied the same way every time, you have a model. When any one of them changes depending on how you feel,
you have an opinion.

Most beginners trade without a model and do not realise it. They mark a zone, watch price approach, and
then decide in the moment whether the approach "looks good". That decision is made under time pressure,
with money at stake, and it is heavily influenced by whether they want the trade to work. A written model
removes that decision from the moment. You are not deciding whether to trade; you are checking whether your
conditions were met.

Writing a model means being specific enough that someone else could apply it to your chart and reach the
same answer. "I buy at demand zones" is not a model. "On the four-hour chart, I look for a fresh demand
zone that produced a departure of at least X pips, and I act only if a fifteen-minute candle closes back
above the zone's upper boundary, with invalidation below the zone's lower boundary" is closer. It names the
timeframe, the location criteria, the trigger event, and the invalidation. Someone else can check it.

There is a practical benefit to this precision. A vague model cannot be tested, and a model that cannot be
tested cannot be improved. If your rules are specific, you can go back through historical charts and count
how often the trigger appeared, how often the invalidation was hit first, and what the results looked like
across a meaningful sample — real information rather than a feeling.

The third element, and the one most often left out, is the no-trade condition. Every model should state the
circumstances under which you do not act even though the setup appears: a timeframe conflict, a major
economic release imminent, a setup that has already moved too far from the location, or a day when you have
already reached your loss limit for the week. A model without no-trade rules will be applied to every
chart that vaguely resembles it.

## 3. Real Chart Walkthrough

These are hypothetical numbers, used only to show how a model is written and checked. Suppose you have
written this model for GBP/USD on the four-hour and fifteen-minute charts. Location: a fresh demand zone on
the four-hour chart that produced a departure of at least forty pips. Trigger: a fifteen-minute candle
closing back above the upper boundary of that zone. Invalidation: a fifteen-minute close below the lower
boundary of the zone. No-trade conditions: a four-hour chart making lower highs, or a high-impact release
scheduled within the next hour.

Now suppose the four-hour chart shows a demand zone between 1.2520 and 1.2550, and price trades down to
1.2535 during a London session. The zone is fresh, and the departure that formed it was sixty pips, so the
location criteria are met. At 10:15 a fifteen-minute candle closes at 1.2558, above the upper boundary. The
trigger is met. Invalidation sits at 1.2518, below the zone.

At this point the model has fired, and the location, trigger and invalidation are all recorded. What the
model does not tell you is the outcome. Price might have continued upward, and it might equally have
reversed and closed below 1.2518 within the hour, invalidating the idea. Both are ordinary results for a
model of this kind, and a single instance tells you nothing about the model's quality. That is why the next
step in this lesson is testing it across many historical examples rather than judging it from one.

## 4. Common Mistakes

- Writing rules that only you can interpret, such as "when it looks ready".
- Changing the trigger mid-trade so a losing position stays open past its invalidation.
- Testing a model on ten examples, seeing a good result, and treating it as validated.
- Leaving out no-trade conditions, so the model gets applied to every vaguely similar chart.
- Adding conditions after a loss to explain it away, which turns a model into a story.

## 5. The SAGZFX Rule

If someone else cannot check your entry model on your chart and reach the same answer, it is not a model
yet.

## 6. Practice Assignment

Open the SAGZFX Practice Account. It uses virtual money only, no real order is ever placed, and nothing
you do here can earn or lose you real funds.

Turn one chart idea into a written checklist with four parts: the location, the trigger, the invalidation,
and at least two no-trade conditions. Keep each part to one or two sentences and name the timeframes
explicitly.

Then test the checklist on twenty historical examples on the same pair and timeframes. For each example,
record the date, whether the location criteria were met, whether the trigger appeared, and what price did
relative to the invalidation level. Include examples where the trigger never appeared, and count them as
no-trade outcomes rather than discarding them.

*Why this matters:* a rule set that survives twenty honest historical checks, including the examples it
skipped, is the foundation of every later module on risk and trade management, and it is the difference
between a method and a habit.

## 7. Checkpoint Question

**Question:** Which of the following is a complete entry model as defined in this lesson?

- A. Buying at demand zones when the market looks strong.
- B. A fresh four-hour demand zone, a fifteen-minute close above the zone's upper boundary as the trigger,
  and invalidation below the zone's lower boundary.
- C. A zone combined with a fair value gap and a break of structure.
- D. Entering whenever a candle closes above the previous candle's high.

**Correct answer:** B

**Why:** A model needs a location, a specific trigger and an invalidation level; the other options are
either vague, incomplete, or a condition with no location or invalidation attached.
